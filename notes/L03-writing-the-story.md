# 📜 Level 3 — Writing the Story
> 📖 Book pp. 37–56 · ⏱️ ~55 min study · 📱 F2P relevance ★★★☆☆ — Most F2P games run on light story, but meta-narratives, lovable characters, smart naming and story-driven LiveOps are serious retention (and store-page) weapons.

## ⚡ TL;DR — the 30-second version
- **Games don't need a story, but they always produce a narrative**: the order of events the player lives through. Design for the player's narrative, and never confuse story with gameplay.
- **Classic story = hero with a desire → problem → failed attempt → reversal → bigger problem → biggest problem → resolution → reward.** Always a beginning, middle and end. Story is the **salt**, gameplay is the **meat**.
- **Triangle of Weirdness:** of **characters, activities, world**, make only **one** weird, or players won't "get it".
- **Serve three audiences** (story fans, lore divers, skippers): optional depth, story told through gameplay, start late in the action, keep things moving, prefer **suspense over surprise**, skip the amnesia cliché.
- **Endings, titles and characters matter:** end when the player feels satisfied; keep titles short (literal, cool, punny or "purple cow"); give the first act time so players bond with characters before you hurt them; make death matter; build humor and actions from personality; pick names that fit.
- **Kids and licenses:** don't oversimplify for kids (teach sneakily); with licenses, know the IP inside out, find the "no-no"s early, collect every reference, then make it your own.
- 📱 **F2P add-on:** story in mobile is mostly a **meta-layer** (renovation stories like *Homescapes*/*Royal Match*), **character IP** (*Clash*, *Brawl Stars*), and **LiveOps fuel** (episodic chapters, seasonal themes, collabs).

## 1. Story vs Gameplay: The Eternal Designer Fight
**🧠 Big idea:** Designers have argued forever about whether story or gameplay matters more. Rogers' answer: both camps are partly right. A game doesn't *need* a story, yet every game *has* one.

**📋 The details:**
- **Pro-story camp:** games need a story to engage the player; games are an artistic medium for telling stories.
- **Anti-story camp:** story is just how people describe the play afterward; or, more bluntly, story is the thing you watch while the game loads.
- At GDCs worldwide the two sides shout "*Bioshock*!" (story-rich) versus "*Doom*!" (pure action) at each other.
- Rogers calls both sides right *and* wrong: **a game doesn't need a story, and yet it always has one.** The resolution of this riddle comes in Section 4 (story vs narrative).

**🎮 Book examples:** *Bioshock* → the poster child for story-driven games · *Doom* → the poster child for "just let me shoot things".

**📱 F2P mobile translation:** F2P has its own version of this fight: "Does a match-3 even need a story?" Many top games say yes, as a light **meta layer** (*Homescapes*' butler drama), while others thrive with almost none (*Block Blast!*, *Subway Surfers*). Both can be right, depending on audience and retention goals.

**🪝 Make it stick:** Picture a GDC panel turning into a food fight: one table chanting "Bioshock!", the other "Doom!". Rogers walks in with a sign: "You're both wrong. And right. Sit down."

## 2. The Classic Story Structure (9 Steps)
**🧠 Big idea:** The most basic story structure, taught from Aristotle to Hollywood screenwriters, is a hero with a desire who faces escalating problems until a final resolution earns the prize.

**📋 The details:** Rogers illustrates each step with a stick-figure comic: a hero in love with a princess in a tower.

| # | Step | The book's drawing |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | There is a **hero** with a **desire**. | Hero strolls along, heart in his thought bubble; princess waves from her tower. |
| 2 | An **event** throws the hero's life into disarray and blocks the desire → it creates a **problem**. | Lightning strikes and splits the ground into a chasm between hero and tower. |
| 3 | The hero **tries to overcome** the problem… | He crouches to leap the chasm. |
| 4 | …but his **method fails**. | He falls into the pit. |
| 5 | A **reversal of fortune** makes things worse. | He's stuck at the bottom, miserable. |
| 6 | An **even greater problem** puts him at **greater risk**. | Rain floods the pit; he's treading water. |
| 7 | **One last problem** threatens him with the **most risk of all**. | A dragon rises out of the water beside him. |
| 8 | The hero must **resolve the final problem**… | He jumps off the dragon's head to escape. |
| 9 | …to gain his **object of desire**. Happily ever after (until the sequel). | Hero and princess kiss inside a heart. |

The shape of it (risk rising, then released):
```
RISK ▲
     │                                      7 ★ dragon in the flood
     │                              6 rain floods the pit
     │                     5 stuck at the bottom (reversal)
     │             4 falls in (method fails)
     │       3 tries to jump
     │   2 lightning splits the ground (event → problem)
     │ 1 hero ♥ princess (desire)                  8 leaps off the dragon → 9 the kiss ♥
     └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────▶ time
          BEGINNING        │            MIDDLE              │        END
```

**🎮 Book examples:** The stick-figure hero, princess and dragon: a whole story arc in 9 doodles.

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- **Every F2P event can follow this arc in miniature.** Example (hypothetical) for a 7-day renovation event: Day 1 desire (fix the lighthouse for the festival) → Day 2 storm wrecks it (problem) → Day 3 first repair collapses (failure) → Day 4 the supplies get stolen (reversal) → Day 5 the storm returns (bigger risk) → Day 6 a sea monster blocks the harbor (biggest risk, hardest levels) → Day 7 victory + festival reward (desire gained).
- **The arc maps onto difficulty:** escalating problems = escalating level difficulty, with the "boss" (step 7) as the event's hardest stage.
- **Royal Match** frames special rescue levels around the king being in danger: a tiny "problem → resolve" story in one level.

**🪝 Make it stick:** **"Desire, Disaster, Try, Fail, Flip, Worse, Worst, Win, Prize."** Nine beats, nine doodles, one very damp hero.

## 3. Beyond the Formula: Beginning, Middle, End (and Salt)
**🧠 Big idea:** Learn from Hollywood's story craft without being chained to one template, and remember that in an interactive medium the *play* comes first: story should season the gameplay, not drown it.

**📋 The details:**
- **Every story ALWAYS has a beginning, a middle and an end**, whatever it's about.
- Hollywood has spent years analyzing story. Don't reinvent the wheel: borrow their lessons from **screenwriting books, courses and sites**.
- Still, no need to stay shackled to a stock template like **Joseph Campbell's "Hero's Journey"** or **Syd Field's "Three Act Structure."**
- **Borrow structures from other media:**
  - Ask how a **song** tells a story.
  - Or a **TV news report**.
  - Or an ancient **Homeric poem**.
  - Look to **non-Western storytelling** traditions for inspiration.
- **Games are interactive.** Rogers borrows Shakespeare's line from *Hamlet*, "the play's the thing" (and notes Shakespeare understood story pretty well for a guy who never played a video game).
- **Meat and salt:** if gameplay is the **meat** of the game, story is the **salt**. A little adds flavor; too much ruins the dish (and can kill you).
- **Some games have no story at all** and are still engaging: *Tetris*, *Bejeweled*, even *Pac-Man*.

**🎮 Book examples:** Hero's Journey and Three Act Structure → known templates you may use but needn't obey · *Tetris*, *Bejeweled*, *Pac-Man* → story-free but compelling.

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- **Salt levels by genre:** hyper-casual and block puzzles (*Block Blast!*) ≈ no salt; match-3 with meta (*Homescapes*, *Royal Match*) ≈ a pinch between levels; gacha RPGs (*Genshin Impact*, *Honkai: Star Rail*) ≈ heavy seasoning, but the combat and collecting are still the meat.
- **Other-media structures fit mobile well:** a **TV series** (episodic chapters with cliffhangers) is a natural model for LiveOps story; a **sitcom** (same cast, new small problem each episode, nothing permanent changes) fits evergreen casual games; **comic strips** (3–4 panels, a joke and out) fit the 2-line dialog popups between levels.

**🪝 Make it stick:** Story is salt. Nobody orders "a plate of salt with a side of steak."

## 4. Story vs Narrative: The Player Writes Their Own
**🧠 Big idea:** A **story** is what the writer tells; a **narrative** is the sequence of events the player actually experiences. Every play session creates a narrative, so the designer's real job is to make *all* the likely narratives fun.

**📋 The details:**
- **Narrative** literally means **"an order of events."** Humans perceive time as linear, so we describe experiences in order even when there's no traditional story.
- That's why story-less games like *Tetris* still generate narratives. (The book's doodle: one person excitedly describing a *Tetris* board to a friend in a speech bubble.)
- **Every time someone plays, they create a narrative**, and there are infinitely many possible ones.
- As a designer, look at **all of the possible narratives** and figure out how to make them **ALL fun**. Goal: many narratives the player will enjoy.
  - Footnote: realistically, as many as you can predict; it's one giant game of "what if". Since possibilities are endless, predicting everything isn't worth the time. When something unexpected happens that doesn't break the game, designers happily call it a "feature".
- **In a narrative, the player is the hero.** Look at the game from the player's point of view and be aware of the **ordering of events and experiences** that will build their narrative.
- As each experience builds on the last, aim for **rising emotional states**. Then design **systems that choreograph** those experiences, which produce the emotions.
- **Example: *Left 4 Dead*'s "Director" AI** controls pacing:
  - It estimates the player's **"stress level"** from variables including **health, skill and location**.
  - It then adjusts **how many zombies attack**, **which items spawn** (ammo, health), and even **the music**.
  - Result: the game generates a unique, dynamic experience each time.
  - Most developers don't have tech like this, so it's up to the **designer** to craft such situations as well as they can.
- **Know the player's narrative.** It can end up very different from the game's written story.
- **Key rule: never mistake story for gameplay, and never mistake gameplay for story.**

**🎮 Book examples:** *Tetris* → story-free yet narrative-rich · *Left 4 Dead*'s Director → a system that shapes pacing and emotion dynamically.

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- **Players' narratives are your real story in F2P.** "I was one move away, used my last booster, and the cascade won it!" or "Our clan came back in the last hour of the war" are the stories players retell, screenshot and share.
- **Dynamic difficulty is the mobile "Director".** Many F2P puzzle and casual games adjust difficulty or rewards based on player behavior (e.g., near-miss pacing, easier levels after repeated fails). Handle with care and ethically: it should serve the player's emotional arc (tension → relief), not just squeeze purchases.
- **Design the emotional arc of a session:** easy warm-up win → rising challenge → a tense "almost lost it" → big reward → a reason to come back. That's the narrative of a good 5-minute session.
- **Story ≠ gameplay in F2P:** a beautiful renovation story won't save a weak match-3 core, and a great core doesn't need a novel stapled on.

**🪝 Make it stick:** The writer writes the *script*; the player writes the *diary*. Players only retell the diary.

## 5. Almost Anything Can Be Gameplay
**🧠 Big idea:** Don't let subject matter limit you. Nearly any topic can be turned into gameplay, even a simple children's fairy tale.

**📋 The details:**
- Rogers believes **almost ANYTHING can be made into gameplay**. Proof by weird examples:
  - ***Mr. Mosquito***: you're a mosquito drinking a family's blood.
  - ***SimCity***: you build and manage a city.
  - ***PaRappa the Rapper***: a dog raps to win the love of his flower girlfriend.
- **Little Red Riding Hood, turned into a game:**
  1. Red walks through the forest to Grandma's house = **exploration**. Add goodies to collect in her picnic basket (**inventory system**) and a fallen log or two to jump.
  2. Red meets the Big Bad Wolf = **first enemy encounter**. You can't kill him yet (unless you add "wolf minions" as regular enemies).
  3. With a full basket (**gating mechanism**), Red reaches Grandma's house (**next level**) and finds "Grandma" in bed.
  4. Red questions "Grandma's" identity (the "what big eyes" exchange) as a **quiz, a puzzle, or a rhythm mini-game**.
  5. The disguise drops and it's the wolf → **boss fight** to the death!
- Footnotes: in the original tale Red gets eaten and a woodcutter saves her, but why watch a cutscene when you could have a boss fight? (Rogers prefers his ending; boss fights just hadn't been invented yet.) And sure, it wouldn't be a *long* game, but it's a game.

| Story beat | Game element |
|---|---|
| Walk through the forest | Exploration + collectibles + simple platforming |
| Picnic basket | Inventory system |
| Meet the wolf | First enemy encounter (can't win yet) |
| Full basket needed | Gating mechanism |
| Grandma's house | Next level |
| "What big eyes" | Quiz / puzzle / rhythm mini-game |
| Wolf revealed | Boss fight |

**🎮 Book examples:** *Mr. Mosquito*, *SimCity*, *PaRappa* → wild subjects, real games · Little Red Riding Hood → a fairy tale already contains exploration, inventory, enemies, gating, puzzles and a boss.

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- Mobile proves this daily: cooking (*Cooking Fever*-style time managers), cleaning, organizing (sorting and "triple-match" games like *Match Factory!*), parking puzzles, becoming a hole that swallows a city (*Hole.io*), running a hotel or farm (*Hay Day*). If people do it or dream about it, someone has made it a mobile game.
- **Turn any story into an F2P event:** Little Red as a 5-day event: Day 1 collect basket items (collection quest), Day 2 dodge wolf levels (obstacle levels), Day 3 basket full unlocks the cottage (gate), Day 4 "big eyes" quiz mini-game, Day 5 wolf boss level with a big reward chest.

**🪝 Make it stick:** Rogers gave Little Red Riding Hood a boss fight. If a fairy tale about a picnic basket can be a game, so can your weird hobby.

## 6. The Triangle of Weirdness
**🧠 Big idea:** Games can be as strange as your imagination allows, but too much strangeness alienates people. Of the three corners (**characters, activities, world**), make only **one** weird.

**📋 The details:**
- First, recall the famous **production triangle**: **Time, Quality, Money**, and you may **choose two** (fast + good = expensive, etc.).
- Video games, technical limits of the platform aside, are limited only by your imagination: you can build worlds with non-Newtonian physics, bizarre characters and absurd quests.
- But it's possible to **go crazy with creativity**, especially when writing the story. So Rogers created the **Triangle of Weirdness**.
- The three corners: **Characters · Activities · World**.
- **Unlike the production triangle (pick two), you may pick only ONE corner** to make weird. Choose more and you risk **alienating your audience**.

```
     PRODUCTION TRIANGLE                 TRIANGLE OF WEIRDNESS
            Time                              Characters
             /\                                   /\
            /  \                                 /  \
           /pick\                               /pick\
          / TWO  \                             / ONE! \
         /________\                           /________\
    Quality      Money                 Activities      World
```

**Three examples from the book (each with a doodle):**

| Work | Weird corner | What stays familiar |
|---|---|---|
| *The Wizard of Oz* | **Characters**: a Tin Man, a Lion, a Scarecrow | The land of Oz was a fairly typical fairy-tale setting for its time (1900s); the characters' desires are relatable: courage, love, getting home |
| *Star Wars* | **World**: Jawas, Wookiees, Jedi, a cantina full of the galaxy's strangest scum | Characters are familiar archetypes (young hero, princess in distress, charming rogue) with familiar desires (join the war, beat the villains, get the girl) |
| *Monty Python and the Holy Grail* | **Activities**: designing a shrubbery for the Knights Who Say "Ni!", being slaughtered by the killer rabbit of Caerbannog | Archetypal characters (King Arthur and his knights) on a classic quest through medieval England |

- **Going too far:** the films *Dune* (1984) and *The City of Lost Children* (1995), and the games *SkullMonkeys* and *Muscle Man March*: very creative and unique, but many audiences felt they "didn't get it". **Nobody likes to feel stupid.**
- Rogers says all of these **violate the Triangle of Weirdness**. Break it at your own peril.

**🎮 Book examples:** Oz (weird characters), Star Wars (weird world), Holy Grail (weird activities) → each keeps two corners familiar · Dune, City of Lost Children, SkullMonkeys, Muscle Man March → too much weird, audiences lost.

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- **Mobile examples by corner:**
  - **Weird characters:** *Plants vs. Zombies 2* (pea-shooting plants vs. zombies, in an ordinary backyard, doing ordinary "defend the house" tower defense); *Cut the Rope* (the candy-hungry monster Om Nom; the activity is just cutting ropes); *Angry Birds* (wingless furious birds vs. pigs, with a simple slingshot activity).
  - **Weird activity:** *Hole.io* (you're a hole swallowing a normal city, no real characters); *Katamari*-style "roll everything up" ideas.
  - **Weird world:** premium mobile *Monument Valley* (impossible architecture, but a simple character just walking around).
- **Why it matters even more in F2P:** your UA ad has about **3 seconds** to be understood. Weird character + weird world + weird activity = a viewer who scrolls past confused. One weird corner = a hook; three = noise.
- **Theme tests:** if you test themes with ad creatives, the Triangle is a handy checklist for why a "cool" concept tests badly.

**🪝 Make it stick:** The Triangle says **"CAW"**: **C**haracters, **A**ctivities, **W**orld. Only one crow gets to be weird; three weird crows and the audience flies away.

## 7. Three Kinds of Story Audience (and How to Serve Them All)
**🧠 Big idea:** Your players split into three groups by how much they care about story. Keep story **in service of gameplay** and use specific techniques so each group gets what it wants.

**📋 The details:**
- The three types (an observation Rogers credits to **Ken Levine**, director of *Bioshock*, from his 2009 GDC talk):
  1. Players who are **into your story as it happens**.
  2. Players who want to **dig deep into the lore**.
  3. Players who **couldn't care less about the story**.
- Appealing to all three is hard. **Best rule of thumb: the story serves the gameplay, never the other way around.**
- **Tips for weaving story into the game:**
  - **For depth-seekers: provide optional detail that doesn't get in the way** of the main story.
    - *Bioshock* and *Batman: Arkham Asylum* use **non-mandatory collectible audiotapes** that reveal deeper lore without interrupting the main plot.
  - **For ride-along players who mash through:** they'll "A button" past audio cues and cutscenes. So **also reveal the story through gameplay and level design**, or they'll get lost and confused.
    - Footnote: yes, it's technically their fault for skipping, but remember the first rule of responsible design: **love thy player**.
    - You can also turn story into gameplay: **playable flashbacks** or **puzzles**.
  - **Start the story as late into the action as possible:** mid-boss fight, at the end of a level, during a car chase.
    - Works best for games with a traditional story. (Starting *Tetris* with dozens of pieces already raining down is probably not wise.)
  - **Keep the story lively and moving.** Professional screenwriters introduce a **change in plot or action about every 15 minutes**.
    - Even in non-story games, **play sessions are getting "bite-sized"**: short enough to enjoy in brief bursts.

**🎮 Book examples:** Ken Levine's three audience types · *Bioshock* and *Batman: Arkham Asylum* audiotapes → optional depth · playable flashbacks/puzzles → story as play · 15-minute screenwriter rule → pacing.

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- **The three types in mobile:**
  - **Skippers** are the majority in casual games. Every dialog needs a skip button, and the game must still make sense from visuals alone (the renovated room *shows* the progress).
  - **Ride-alongs** enjoy 2–3 short lines between levels with expressive character art. Keep text tiny.
  - **Divers** want lore: character profiles, collection books, codex entries, side stories in events (a common pattern in gacha games like *Genshin Impact* and *Honkai: Star Rail*).
- **Story through environment = the renovation meta.** In *Homescapes* or *Royal Match*, you don't need to read anything to understand "this room was broken, now it's beautiful because of me".
- **Start late = FTUE starts with play.** Mobile players should be playing within seconds; save the story setup for after the first win.
- **15-minute rule → the "something new every session" rule.** Mobile sessions are often just a few minutes, so introduce a new blocker, area, character line, reward or event beat regularly. Match-3 games, for instance, introduce new obstacles and mechanics across their level map to keep things fresh.
- Rogers' 2010 remark that sessions are becoming "bite-sized" basically predicted mobile.

**🪝 Make it stick:** Three kinds of story fans: **Surfers** (ride the wave), **Divers** (go deep), **Skippers** (*tap tap tap* → "where's the next level?"). Build a beach that works for all three.

## 8. Surprise, Suspense and Stakes
**🧠 Big idea:** You don't need clever twists; predictable stories delight when the *how* is fun. Avoid the amnesia cliché, prefer **suspense** (show the bomb) over **surprise**, and choose stakes that aren't always "save the world".

**📋 The details:**

**A. The twist ending is overrated**
- Rogers blames Hollywood for the trend of **twist/surprise endings**. Being surprised can be fun, but **predictable endings have merit**.
- Remember **wish fulfillment** (from Level 2): people *like* it when the good guys win and the bad guys lose, even though that's the oldest story there is.
- **James Bond example (1980s films):** before even seeing one, young Rogers knew Bond would get a cool gadget, drive an awesome car, foil the villain, save the world and get the girl. So why go? Because the fun was in the twists and turns: **he knew the WHO, the WHAT and the WHY, but not the HOW.**
- **Predicting outcomes feels good**: players feel smart, as if they correctly predicted an election result or cracked a whodunit. Life is unpredictable enough; give the audience some predictability.
- Takeaway: **you don't have to be especially clever when writing a story, just entertaining.**

**B. Amnesia: the #1 cliché**
- Rogers ranks **amnesia** as the **number one video game story cliché** of all time.
- He understands why writers use it: it mirrors the player's own lack of knowledge about the character and world at the start.
- But it becomes an excuse for an **unreliable narrator** to hide information on purpose, just to set up a **shock ending**. It feels forced and **isn't fair to the player**.

**C. Hitchcock: suspense beats surprise**
- **Surprise version:** two men sit at a table talking about baseball for about five minutes, then a bomb suddenly explodes. The audience is shocked, but the surprise lasts about **15 seconds**.
- **Suspense version:** show the audience the **bomb under the table**, set to go off in **five minutes**. Now the baseball chat is agony; viewers squirm, silently begging the two men to leave
- Letting viewers **in on the danger** makes them **emotionally invested**. By the climax you've built far more excitement than a surprise blast ever could.
- Footnote: Hitchcock also suggests *not* actually killing the characters in that scene. In a game, that would just be game over!

**D. What's at stake?**
- Many games are about **saving the world** from evil or destruction. But not every movie is a blockbuster, and not every game needs world-ending stakes.
- **Small themes can matter as much as big ones.**
- Rogers argues it's worth aiming for a theme beyond "**violence solves all problems**"; *Frogger*, *Zoo Tycoon* and *Braid* all pulled it off.

**🎮 Book examples:** 1980s James Bond films → known WHO/WHAT/WHY, fun HOW · amnesia → cliché and unfair · Hitchcock's bomb → suspense > surprise · *Frogger*, *Zoo Tycoon*, *Braid* → stakes and themes beyond violence.

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- **Predictability is the F2P comfort zone.** Casual players love knowing the shape of things (finish the room, the room becomes gorgeous; finish the event, get the big chest). The fun is the *how*: which levels, which boosters, which near-miss.
- **Suspense is built into F2P UI:** visible **countdown timers** on events, a final-hour leaderboard race, a chest that's 90% full. In *Candy Crush Saga*, **candy bombs** show a countdown number, so you *see* the danger ticking down: Hitchcock's bomb, as a puzzle mechanic.
- **Cliffhangers as retention:** ending a story chapter with a visible threat (a storm approaching, a rival arriving) creates "bomb under the table" tension that pulls players back next session.
- **The "new arrival" device replaces amnesia.** Mobile games usually cast the player as the newcomer (new mayor, new commander, new manager), which justifies not knowing things *without* hiding info from the player.
- **Small stakes win in casual:** fixing up a family mansion (*Homescapes*), restoring a garden (*Gardenscapes*), building a town (*Township*). Nobody needs to save the galaxy to enjoy a coffee-break game.

**🪝 Make it stick:** Surprise is a firecracker: bang, gone in 15 seconds. Suspense is a lit fuse you can *see*: five minutes of squirming. In F2P, the fuse is the event timer.

## 9. Time to Wrap It Up (Endings and Length)
**🧠 Big idea:** Endings are as hard as beginnings. End the game when the player feels satisfied and has accomplished what they needed to, then give them reasons to stay in a world they love.

**📋 The details:**
- **Early games had no ending**, only a **kill screen**, or they ran forever with scores wrapping around like a car odometer.
  - Footnote: a **kill screen** wasn't a designed game-over screen; it came from a programming bug or oversight. Most famous: **level 256 of *Pac-Man***, where a bug garbles half the screen so you can't collect all the dots or clear the board. Arcade owners had to unplug ("kill") the machine to restart it.
- Then **Dragon's Lair** arrived and everyone wanted to know whether Dirk rescued the princess. **That's where game storytelling begins.**
- **How long should a game be?**
  - "Old days": about **20 hours** on average; "really old days": about **40**.
  - Now (2010): most games average **8–10 hours**.
  - But it depends on the game: after 8 hours of *Fallout 3* you've barely scratched the surface.
- **Rogers' rule: end the game when the player feels satisfied.**
  - You may leave plot threads dangling to encourage **replay** or set up **sequels**, but **play fair**: let players feel they accomplished everything they needed to.
- **Post-story extras** let players keep playing without sitting through the same story again:
  - **multiple endings · minigames · unlockable and downloadable content · "deleted levels"** (like deleted scenes on a DVD).
- **If you create a world the player wants to play in, they'll come back to play.**
- **Writing the story last:** some teams focus solely on gameplay and bolt the story on at the end. They say it works; Rogers admits it would make him nervous.

**🎮 Book examples:** *Pac-Man*'s level-256 kill screen · *Dragon's Lair* → birth of game story · *Fallout 3* → long games need more than 8 hours · DVD-style deleted levels.

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- **F2P games are designed *not* to end.** The "length" is measured in months or years of retention, not hours. When players hit the end of the content (a "content cliff"), many churn, so live games keep adding levels, areas and events.
- **But every *chapter* still needs a satisfying ending.** Each event, season or story chapter should close its loop (the room is finished, the season boss is beaten) even if the overall saga continues. Playing fair still matters.
- **Post-story extras = LiveOps.** Rogers' list (minigames, unlockables, extra content) is basically today's events, side modes, seasonal passes and collection albums.
- **"A world players want to play in" is the core of retention.** It's also why *Clash of Clans* bases and *Hay Day* farms feel like home: players return to *their* place.
- **Story last is common in F2P** (events are often themed late), and Rogers' nervousness is justified: bolted-on stories feel disconnected from the gameplay. Involve narrative early in event design.

> ⚠️ **2010 → 2026:** "8–10 hours" made sense for $60 boxed games. In F2P, length is open-ended and measured by retention curves (D1/D7/D30 and beyond). What still holds: players need satisfying *closure points*, and fair endings to arcs.

**🪝 Make it stick:** Old arcade games ended when the code broke. Modern F2P games never end, but each chapter still needs its "and they lived happily ever after… until next season."

## 10. Naming Your Game
**🧠 Big idea:** The title is one of the most important parts of your game's story and marketing. Pick a title type on purpose, keep it short, name it early, and check it's legally available.

**📋 The details:**

**A. Four types of title**

| Type | What it is | Book examples | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Literal** | Says where it came from: the **main character** (*Sonic the Hedgehog*, *Voodoo Vince*), the **main location** (*Castle Wolfenstein*, *Saints Row*), or a **gameplay activity/component** (*Command & Conquer*, *Boom Blocks*) | see left | Can be bland, but it's clear |
| **Action / "cool"** | Captures the game's spirit without naming characters or places | *Darksiders*, *Brütal Legend*, *Gears of War* | Must actually feel like the game |
| **Punny** | Makes you appreciate the clever wordplay | *Just Cause*, *Half-Life*, *System Shock* | Players may not get the reference or find it funny; humor is subjective |
| **"Purple cow"** | Makes people stop and stare and wonder *why* that name; after playing they understand | *LittleBigPlanet*, *Far Cry*, *Resident Evil* | Big upside: once a word is tied to your game, others can't use it without evoking you (good luck naming your game "Halo" now) |

- Footnote: "purple cow" comes from a short humorous poem by **Gelett Burgess** about never having seen a purple cow.

**B. Shorter is better**
- **Short titles are easier to remember and to say.** Rogers likes **two or three syllables**: *Star Wars*, *Don-key Kong*, *Pac-Man*, *Ha-lo*.
- He traces this to **arcade marquees**, which had to grab attention, create mystery and describe the gameplay in a small space.
- ***Defender*** is still one of the best game names ever: one three-syllable word that sums up the whole game.
- **Short titles make better logos** for the start screen and the box cover. Consider the **marketing** side.
- If you need a longer title or subtitle, still keep it tight:
  - ***Uncharted: Drake's Fortune*** combines two "purple cows": who is Drake, and what's his fortune? "Uncharted" evokes exploring dangerous unknown territory.
  - ***Batman: Arkham Asylum*** tells you who you play and where it happens.

**C. Name it early, and check it's free**
- **Name your game sooner rather than later.** Rogers prefers titles the developers created over ones invented by others; wait too long and the **publisher's marketing department** will name it for you (and you won't like it).
- Your perfect title may already be **in use or trademarked**. Check with the publisher's **legal department** before you get attached; even a quick internet search helps as a first step.

**🎮 Book examples:** listed in the table above, plus *Defender* (perfect one-word name), *Uncharted: Drake's Fortune* (double purple cow), *Batman: Arkham Asylum* (who + where), *Halo* (word now owned by the game).

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- **Mobile title types:**
  - Literal activity/component: *Block Blast!*, *Match Factory!*, *Merge Mansion*, *Subway Surfers*, *Hill Climb Racing*, *Coin Master*.
  - Literal location: *Township*.
  - Literal character/IP: *Cookie Run: Kingdom*, *Pokémon GO*.
  - Action/cool: *Clash Royale*, *Brawl Stars*, *Squad Busters*, *Whiteout Survival*.
  - Punny/wordplay: *Homescapes* and *Gardenscapes* (home/garden + "-scapes").
  - Purple cow: *Among Us* (wait, among *whom*?).
- **Store constraints make short even more important:** store app names have tight character limits (around 30 characters on both major stores), and home-screen labels cut long names off. The icon plus a 1–3 word name has to work at thumbnail size.
- **ASO twist:** many mobile games put a searchable keyword in the subtitle or after a colon (Name: Keyword), balancing a memorable brand with discoverability.
- **Check availability on the stores too:** search both stores for name collisions and trademark conflicts before falling in love. Also check how the name sounds and what it means in your key localization languages.

**🪝 Make it stick:** Titles come in **"LAP-Cow"** flavors: **L**iteral, **A**ction/cool, **P**unny, purple **Cow**. And a great title fits on an arcade marquee, or today, under an app icon.

## 11. Creating Characters
**🧠 Big idea:** Players need a **first act** to bond with a character before anything bad happens to them. Make deaths matter by making characters important, find humor and gameplay in personality and profession, know their motivations, and choose names that fit.

**📋 The details:**

**A. Don't skip the first act**
- Designer **Andy Ashcraft** believes game developers only care about telling the **second act** of the story, and Rogers agrees.
- In games, **act 2 is the grind** toward **act 3**, the conclusion (usually the last level and a boss fight).
- Developers skip **act 1** to "cut to the chase", covering it with a **cutscene** or, worse, the **manual** (which nobody reads; Rogers jokes that game manuals were rarely worth reading anyway). Footnote reminder: all stories have a beginning, middle and end.
- **Act 1 is where players learn about the character and start to care.** You need it for the bond, even if you later kill the character over and over (common in games) or turn them into a killing machine.

**B. The *Robocop* lesson**
- In the film, we meet police officer **Alex Murphy**: an honest cop and decent guy fighting crime in a futuristic Detroit. By about page 25 of the script you care about him, so it hurts when criminals gun him down.
- Act 2 begins when Murphy is rebuilt as the cyborg Robocop.
- In all the game adaptations, Murphy's death was just a **cutscene**; players started as Robocop and began blasting immediately.
- Rogers' hypothetical fix: **level 1 plays as Murphy the human cop**, tracking criminals and ending with his death. The player bonds with him, so the death and resurrection hit far harder.
- Footnote: Rogers knows why the original developers did it: (1) playing a criminal-blasting cyborg is more appealing than a fragile human; (2) 1988 game cartridges lacked memory for two completely different player models, especially for a one-level human; (3) the title promises Robocop, so nobody wants to open as the man who gets shot first.

**C. Make death mean something**
- Death should matter, **especially when it's not the main character**. (When did a game death last make you cry, besides deleting your save file?)
- **You must care about a character before killing them means anything.**
- Making the victim a **relative** or partner is *not* a shortcut, especially if they die in the opening cutscene. Rogers recalls a game where the hero's relative was murdered after just one level together; he felt none of the hero's righteous anger, because he barely knew the guy.
- **Invest time in characters, even if the plan is to kill them off.**

**D. Even better: make the character important to the PLAYER (gameplay-wise)**
- A character can:
  - give **information on how to play**;
  - **be the economy system** (e.g., the shopkeeper);
  - regularly provide **health power-ups**.
- Remove that character and the player *feels* the loss in their gameplay, not just the plot.
- **Aeris in *Final Fantasy VII*** (spoiler!): Rogers calls her the only game death that truly felt impactful, because she filled many roles: damsel in distress, part of the hero's love triangle, a character contributing solutions to plot problems, and a **playable party member**. Her loss was felt on many levels.
- **Build characters up before you tear them down. Make their loss count.**
- **Non-human companions work too:** the deaths of **Agro the horse** (*Shadow of the Colossus*) and **Dogmeat the dog** (*Fallout 3*) hit players similarly. What matters is the **bond**. (The book's doodle: a gravestone labeled "your character".)

**E. Humor comes from character**
- Most writers agree **comedy is harder than drama**. Rogers believes **the secret to humor is character**.
- ***Team Fortress 2*** is one of the funniest games he's played, yet it has no physical gags, "hilarious" jokes, or burp-and-fart humor.
- It's funny because the **characters are so true to themselves**: the wry **Australian sniper** defending his job to his parents; the **Russian heavy** who's a little too fond of his "sandvich".
- Their **appearance, animations and voice barks** all reinforce their personalities, lifting them from stereotypes to original, funny characters.

**F. Let profession and personality drive actions**
- If a character is defined by a **profession or activity**, most of what they do should relate to it.
  - A **demolitions expert** won't negotiate their way into a prison to free a friend: they'll **BLOW IT UP**. They might blow up a door rather than turn the knob.
  - ***Maximo***: the team decided he was **impatient**, always rushing to rescue the princess or get into a fight. He doesn't even stop to open treasure chests; he **kicks them open**, grabs the loot and moves on.
- **Start from personality and you get interesting animations and gameplay.**

**G. Know their motivations**
- Characters have many possible motivations: **success, revenge, love, acceptance, escape, hunger, responsibility, knowledge**.
- A single character can hold several at once, and they often pull in opposite directions.
- Knowing them tells you **what the character will do and say** → a much richer character.

**H. Naming characters**
- Names carry great weight. Would you call a strapping barbarian hero **Mortimer**? Only in a parody or comedy. (The book's doodle has a barbarian proudly explaining "Mortimer" contains "mort", meaning death.)
- **Baby-name books** are a great starting point.
- Rogers likes names with **significance to the character's personality or occupation**. *Star Wars* has great ones: **Luke Skywalker** sounds simple and homespun (a farm boy) while "Skywalker" hints at his yearning to walk among the stars.
  - Footnote: Luke's original surname was **Starkiller**. Lesson: **never give a character a name that gives away the ending of your story!**
- **Juxtaposed names** are fun: in *When Harry Met Sally*, **Harry Burns** (curmudgeonly, burned out) vs **Sally Allbright** (positive, romantic, a little naive). Obvious, but an instant personality snapshot.
- **Comedic characters need fitting names too:** **Guybrush Threepwood**, **SpongeBob SquarePants**, **Larry Laffer** aren't heroic names, but they're perfect for their humorous games.
- **Rule of thumb: when you look at the character's image, the name should fit what you see.**

**🎮 Book examples:** *Robocop* (skipping act 1) · a game killing a relative after one level (no bond) · Aeris in *FFVII* (many roles = real loss) · Agro and Dogmeat (animal bonds) · *Team Fortress 2* (humor from character) · demolitions expert and *Maximo* (personality-driven actions) · Luke Skywalker/Starkiller, Harry Burns/Sally Allbright, Guybrush Threepwood, SpongeBob, Larry Laffer (names).

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- **FTUE = act 1.** Use the first sessions to make players like your mascot or helper: a few warm lines, a funny animation, a small shared win. *Candy Crush Saga*'s Tiffi and Mr. Toffee, *Homescapes*' butler Austin, *Royal Match*'s King Robert all exist so players have a face to root for.
- **Characters important to gameplay = the F2P helper roles.** The tutorial guide who explains features, the shopkeeper who fronts your daily deals, the character who hands out the login reward. When they're charming, the economy feels friendly instead of transactional.
- **Death in F2P works differently.** Collectible heroes that players invested time or money in usually stay playable whatever the story does, because taking them away would feel like theft. Emotional "loss" in F2P is usually softer: a beloved NPC leaving for an event arc, a rival's betrayal, a destroyed area that the player rebuilds.
- **Humor from character drives F2P marketing.** Supercell's characters (*Clash of Clans*' Barbarian, the *Clash Royale* King emotes) carry personality through animation and short animated videos rather than written jokes: that's the *TF2* lesson.
- **Profession → kit.** In *Brawl Stars*, **Dynamike** is a miner who throws dynamite and **Barley** is a bartender-robot who lobs bottles: identity and moveset match, so players read the character instantly.
- **Motivations fuel events:** a character's revenge, escape or love subplot gives each LiveOps event a reason to exist.
- **Names in F2P must read at tiny size and travel:** short, pronounceable in many languages, checked for unfortunate meanings in key markets.

**🪝 Make it stick:** Remember the 8 motivations: **"Some Really Lonely Aliens Eat Hot Rocks Kindly"** = **S**uccess, **R**evenge, **L**ove, **A**cceptance, **E**scape, **H**unger, **R**esponsibility, **K**nowledge. And never name the barbarian Mortimer (unless you want laughs).

> ⚠️ **2010 → 2026:** "The manual nobody reads" is extinct; today the equivalent sin is front-loading lore in a skippable intro video. Same fix: put act 1 in *playable* form.

## 12. A Few Pointers on Writing for Kids of All Ages
**🧠 Big idea:** Kids' game stories don't have to be simple or bad. Children's classics handle complex themes, and good kids' entertainment teaches without anyone noticing.

**📋 The details:**
- Writing for kids doesn't mean the story has to stink.
- **The most common mistake: making the story too simple.** Developers resist complex ideas and themes "because it's for kids".
- But classic children's books like ***Where the Wild Things Are***, ***Heidi*** and ***The Chronicles of Narnia*** handle **layered themes, relationships and big feelings**. Kids' books can carry that weight, so kids' games can too.
- **"Coming of age" means more than the hero finally getting to carry a sword.**
- **Teach without them noticing** (this applies to all games): not "edutainment", but the kind of learning you used to get from movies and comics.
  - Rogers remembers one *Batman* comic that taught him about **1920s comedians**, **African masks**, the opera ***Pagliacci***, **1920s comedians**, and how **paraffin wax changes color as it ages**. Not bad for a "kiddie" story.
- **Feel free to educate while you entertain.** Players may learn something while having fun, and so might you while writing.

**🎮 Book examples:** *Where the Wild Things Are*, *Heidi*, *Narnia* → complex kids' literature · Rogers' *Batman* comic → stealth education.

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- **"Kids of all ages" = the whole casual audience.** Many casual mobile players are adults who enjoy cartoon art; simple *presentation* is fine, but don't make *themes* or *puzzles* dumb.
- **Stealth learning works in mobile:** *Pokémon GO* gets people walking and exploring their neighborhoods; *Duolingo* shows how much learning people accept when it's wrapped in streaks and rewards; trivia and word games build vocabulary without feeling like school.
- **Kids-specific reminder:** games aimed at children face extra privacy and advertising rules (e.g., COPPA in the US), and monetization aimed at kids needs special ethical care (see L02).

**🪝 Make it stick:** Narnia has a lion who dies and comes back. Your kids' game can handle more than "collect 10 apples".

## 13. Writing for Licenses
**🧠 Big idea:** A licensed game uses someone else's intellectual property. You must respect the licensor's brand rules, but a good partnership leaves room for creativity, and it never hurts to ask for more.

**📋 The details:**

**A. Licensing vocabulary**
- **Licensed game:** a game based on a pre-existing **intellectual property (IP)**: a character or world from a movie, comic, real life, TV or even another game.
- **Property:** a licensed character/world (e.g., *Star Wars*, *Batman*, *Harry Potter*, *SpongeBob SquarePants*).
- **Licensee:** the publisher or developer who **pays a fee** to use the property for one or more games.
- **Licensor:** the owner of the original property (e.g., **Lucasfilm, DC Comics, J.K. Rowling, Nickelodeon**).
- **Public domain** footnote: many IPs are free to use: *Dracula*, *Dante's Inferno*, *Cinderella*, *The Three Musketeers*, *The Wizard of Oz*, *The Bible*. Base your work on the **original material**, not on someone else's (possibly protected) interpretation.

**B. Brand limitations**
- Paying for a license doesn't let you make any game you want. The licensee must **work with the licensor to stay on brand**.
- Example: a licensor may not want its character to **kill** enemies, so you design around these **"brand limitations"**.
- Some licensors are **lenient**, others **very strict**.

**C. It never hurts to ask**
- Rogers worked on a license that already had **10+ games**. Not wanting "another installment", the team pitched the licensor a game in a **different genre** than the usual platformer.
- The licensor admitted they were tired of the same style too, but had never thought of changing direction. The team got **more freedom** than expected and made a solid game.
- Lesson: don't assume a license locks you into "same old, same old".

**D. Rogers' five tips for licensed games**
1. **Know the license inside and out.** Read, watch and play everything. Go deep: using less obvious directions or more obscure characters wins over fans. In Rogers' view a licensed game is a love letter to the property: a **celebration** of it and a heartfelt **thank-you to its fans**.
2. **Uncover the "big issues" early.** Ask the licensor about the major **no-nos** to save headaches and avoid making assets you'll have to redo.
   - Example: a game used **hamburgers** as an invulnerability power-up. One playable main character was (though never mentioned in the show) a **vegetarian**; the show's creators objected to their character "eating meat", so the power-up had to change.
3. **Get as much material from the licensor as possible.** TV shows have **"bibles"** (detailed documents on characters and worlds). Long-running comics have years of **back issues**. If the property is still in production (e.g., a movie releasing alongside your game), get **scripts, animatics, storyboards and production photos** ASAP.
4. **For movie licenses, visit the set** if possible and take your own **reference photos**: anything that helps you replicate the license's world closely.
5. **Respect the license, but put your own stamp on it.** A two-hour film may lack the material for an 8–10 hour game, so **work with the licensor to expand the fiction** and fill the gaps. Bring your own interests; they may fit the brand better than you'd think.

- Rogers closes the chapter by heading on to the foundations of gameplay: the **Three Cs** (next level).

**🎮 Book examples:** *Star Wars*/Lucasfilm, *Batman*/DC, *Harry Potter*/J.K. Rowling, *SpongeBob*/Nickelodeon (property and licensor pairs) · public domain stories · the 10-game license that switched genre · the vegetarian-hamburger problem · TV bibles, comic back issues, film set visits.

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- **Licensed F2P is big business:** *Marvel Snap* (Marvel), *Pokémon GO* and *Pokémon TCG Pocket* (Pokémon), *Monopoly GO!* (Hasbro's Monopoly), *Dragon Ball Legends* (Dragon Ball), *Star Wars: Galaxy of Heroes* (Star Wars), *Harry Potter: Hogwarts Mystery* (Harry Potter).
- **Brand limitations hit monetization too:** a licensor may have opinions on gacha, ads, pricing, how characters are shown in offers, or what can be sold. Find these no-nos *before* designing the economy (Rogers' tip 2 applies doubly).
- **Approval cycles shape LiveOps:** every new asset, event and offer may need licensor approval, so licensed live games plan content further ahead.
- **Expanding the fiction is the norm:** a live game runs for years, far longer than any film, so original characters, variants and side stories (worked out *with* the licensor) fill the calendar.
- **Collabs are short licenses:** limited-time crossover events (common in games like *PUBG Mobile* and *Brawl Stars*) follow the same rules in miniature: know the IP, celebrate the fans, clear the no-nos early.
- **Your own IP can be licensed internally:** *Squad Busters* is Supercell crossing over characters from its own games: brand rules still apply, just from your own studio.
- **Public domain as cheap IP:** fairy tales, mythology and classic literature give instant familiarity with zero license fees, and can help satisfy the Triangle of Weirdness (familiar characters, one weird twist).

**🪝 Make it stick:** Licensed design = cooking in someone else's kitchen: use their recipes (bible), ask about allergies first (the vegetarian burger), then add your own spice with the owner's blessing. License tips: **"Deep, Dealbreakers, Docs, Set, Spin"**.

## 14. 📱 F2P Bonus: Narrative in F2P Mobile
*(Not in the 2010 book: the modern mobile layer on Rogers' story principles.)*

**🧠 Big idea:** In F2P mobile, story rarely sits in front of gameplay. It works as a **meta-narrative** that gives the core loop meaning, as **character IP** that players love and marketing sells, and as **LiveOps fuel** that gives every update a reason to log in.

**📋 The details:**

**A. Meta-narratives: the renovation model**
- **How it works:** play a level of the core (match-3) → earn a currency (stars) → spend it on a **story task** (fix the stairs, choose a new sofa) → a short dialog moves the plot → a new task opens → repeat.
- **Examples:** *Homescapes* (Austin the butler restores his family's mansion), *Gardenscapes* (the same butler restores a garden), *Royal Match* (King Robert's castle areas get built and decorated).
- **Why it works (Rogers' terms):**
  - **Story serves gameplay**: the core loop stays king; the story gives each win a visible purpose.
  - **Story through environment**: skippers still see the world transform.
  - **Small stakes**: a house, not the galaxy (Section 8).
  - **Suspense and cliffhangers**: a new problem each chapter (a leak, a rival, a mystery guest) pulls players onward.
  - **Choices = expression**: picking décor styles adds LeBlanc's "Expression" fun (L02).
- ⚠️ **Ethics note:** some mobile ads have shown story scenarios or mini-puzzles that barely appear in the actual game, and advertising regulators (e.g., in the UK) have ruled against misleading game ads. Make sure what you advertise is what players get.

**B. Character-driven IP**
- Strong characters make games **recognizable at thumbnail size**, power ads and merch, and can spread across multiple games.
- **Supercell** is the textbook case: characters from *Clash of Clans* reappear in *Clash Royale* and *Squad Busters*, and short animated videos sell personality without words.
- **Brawl Stars**: every brawler has a look, profession-matching kit, voice lines and lore: Rogers' "start from personality" in action.
- **Gacha games** (*Genshin Impact*, *Honkai: Star Rail*, *AFK Journey*) monetize characters directly, so writing makes players *want* a character before the banner opens. Here narrative literally drives revenue.

**C. Story as retention and LiveOps fuel**
- **Episodic content:** gacha games like *Genshin Impact* and *Honkai: Star Rail* release new story chapters and regions in regular version updates, like a TV season.
- **Seasonal themes:** battle/season passes are usually wrapped in a theme or mini-story, so the reward track feels like a journey.
- **Events as mini-stories:** use the 9-step arc (Section 2) across an event's days.
- **Characters with motivations** generate endless event premises (a rival's revenge, a festival, a character's escape).
- **Cadence matters:** like the screenwriter's 15-minute rule, live games need a regular "something new" beat to avoid stagnation.

**D. Licensed mobile games**
- Quick wins: built-in awareness (lower ad costs from recognizable IP) and fans eager to play.
- Costs: license fees/revenue share, brand limitations, approval cycles, and the risk that fans expect more than a reskin.
- Rogers' rule still decides success: make it a **celebration and a "thank you" to the fans**, not a logo pasted on a generic loop.

**E. Light-narrative toolkit for F2P (checklist)**
- [ ] Can a player who skips every dialog still understand what's happening?
- [ ] Is every story beat tied to a gameplay action or reward?
- [ ] Are dialogs 1–3 short lines with expressive art?
- [ ] Is the FTUE playable within seconds (start late in the action)?
- [ ] Does each chapter/event end with satisfying closure?
- [ ] Does the setup pass the Triangle of Weirdness (one weird corner)?
- [ ] Is there visible suspense (a timer, a threat, a near-complete goal)?
- [ ] Do characters' names, looks and actions match their personalities?

**🪝 Make it stick:** In F2P, story is the frosting, characters are the sprinkles, LiveOps is the bakery that sells a new cupcake every week, and the core loop is still the cake.

## 🏆 Universal Truths & Clever Ideas
1. **Some games need a story, some don't. Every game needs gameplay.**
   📱 so for F2P: *Block Blast!* thrives with no story; *Homescapes* uses one as a meta-layer. Neither works without a great core loop.
2. **A story always has a beginning, a middle and an end.**
   📱 so for F2P: even endless games need chapters, events and seasons that each open, build and close.
3. **Never mistake story for gameplay.**
   📱 so for F2P: a lovely renovation plot can't rescue a weak match-3 core; fix the loop first.
4. **Almost ANYTHING can be made into gameplay.**
   📱 so for F2P: sorting, parking, cooking, cleaning, being a hole: if people do it or dream it, it can be a mobile game.
5. **Create a world the player wants to play in, and they'll come back to play.**
   📱 so for F2P: this is retention in one sentence: players return to *their* base, farm, mansion or crew.
6. **Make death matter.**
   📱 so for F2P: you rarely kill collectible heroes, but loss, departure and rebuilding still create emotion, as long as players were bonded first.
7. **Keep names short and descriptive.**
   📱 so for F2P: the title must work under an app icon and inside store character limits; character names must read in tiny UI and travel across languages.
8. **Don't underestimate kids: they're smarter than you think.**
   📱 so for F2P: simple presentation, smart content; and extra care with kids' privacy and monetization.
9. **Honor the license, but add your own spin.**
   📱 so for F2P: celebrate the fans, clear the no-nos (including monetization) early, and expand the fiction with the licensor for years of LiveOps.

## 🎯 Side quests (5–15 min each)
1. **Nine-Doodle Story (10 min):** Draw the 9-step structure as stick figures for a match-3 event about saving a bakery. One doodle per step.
2. **Triangle Audit (10 min):** Pick 5 games on your phone. For each, mark which corner (characters, activities, world) is weird, if any. Find one that breaks the rule and decide whether it suffers for it.
3. **Little Red Remix (15 min):** Turn another fairy tale (Jack and the Beanstalk, the Three Little Pigs) into a 5-beat game outline with exploration, inventory, gating, a mini-game and a boss.
4. **Title Forge (10 min):** For one game idea, write one title of each type: literal, action/cool, punny, purple cow. Keep each to 2–3 syllables if you can, then search the stores for collisions.
5. **Bomb Under the Table (10 min):** Redesign a surprise moment in a game you know as suspense: what does the player get to see coming, and for how long?
6. **Name Fit Test (10 min):** Sketch (or describe) 3 characters, give each a name, and show them to a friend. Does the name fit what they see?
7. **Skipper Test (15 min):** Play a story-meta mobile game and skip every dialog. Write down whether you still understand the goal and what told you.

## ⚔️ Main quest (homework)
**Quest: Write a Light Meta-Narrative for an F2P Game (≈2–3 hours)**
