# 💡 Level 2 — Ideas
> 📖 Book pp. 23–36 · ⏱️ ~45 min study · 📱 F2P relevance ★★★★☆ — F2P lives or dies on picking the right idea for the right audience and killing the un-fun fast; this chapter is your idea-factory manual.

## ⚡ TL;DR — the 30-second version
- **Great ideas often sound dumb at first** (a yellow dot-eater chased by ghosts?). Never bin an idea just because it sounds silly out loud.
- **Feed your brain unusual inputs**: read outside your bubble, walk/shower, go to talks, play *bad* games, follow your passions. Most "innovation" is a smart remix of what came before.
- **Gamers want good games.** Know your audience (casual vs hardcore, and *age*) early, and give players wish fulfillment: let them feel powerful, smart, sneaky, rich, heroic.
- **"Fun" is subjective and unguaranteed.** Use the **Theory of Un-Fun**: start with a fun core, then keep finding and cutting whatever isn't fun. Don't be too precious with ideas.
- **Brainstorm properly** (brain, pen, paper, place, people; no bad ideas; push to the ridiculous), then **get merciless**: cut, and test your ideas on someone with no stake in them.
- 📱 **F2P add-on:** validate before you build. In mobile, the modern "back of the box" is a store page + a 15-second ad, and the modern "wife-o-meter" is a cheap creative test and a stranger playtest.

## 0. Intro — "So… what do you actually DO?"
**🧠 Big idea:** Outsiders don't understand what a designer does; the designer's raw material is the *idea*, and a game is only as good as the idea fed into the machine.

**📋 The details:**
- The book opens with a cartoon of a typical party chat: someone assumes the designer *programs* the game ("No, I design it"), then assumes they *draw* the characters ("No, the artist does that"), then gives up: "So what do you do?" … "Apparently nothing." → The designer's job is invisible because it lives in decisions and ideas, not in code or art.
- Rogers' running gag: games are made by **elves**. Park a design idea in the centre of the office at night and by morning the elves have built the finished game (footnote: the elves also do shoes, toys and cookies).
- The catch: the elves only work if you leave out a **good** idea. So the real question of the chapter is: *where do good ideas come from?*

**📱 F2P mobile translation:** In a F2P studio, "the designer" may be a game designer, economy designer, level designer, LiveOps designer or product manager. All of them sell ideas *and* the reasons behind them (the player need, the KPI it moves). If you can't explain the "why", people will think you do "apparently nothing" too.

**🪝 Make it stick:** The elves are real, they're called "the rest of the team", and they don't work for free or for bad ideas.

## 1. Ideas: Where to Get Them and Where to Stick Them
**🧠 Big idea:** Good game ideas can come from anywhere and usually sound stupid at first. Most great designs are not "original" lightning strikes: they're smart steps forward on top of earlier games.

**📋 The details:**

**A. Stupid-sounding ideas make money**
- Filmmaker Michel Gondry (director of *Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind*, *The Science of Sleep*, *Be Kind Rewind*) gets credit for the idea that every good idea sits right next to a stupid one.
- Try saying these out loud, the way an outsider would:
  - A yellow blob eats dots while ghosts chase it → *Pac-Man*.
  - A plumber stomps on mushroom heads to find his girlfriend → *Super Mario*.
  - A prince rebuilds the stars by rolling junk into ever-bigger junk balls → *Katamari Damacy*.
- All three sound ridiculous and all three made huge amounts of money.
- **Lesson:** never dismiss a game idea just because it sounds dumb. (Footnote trick: when someone calls your idea stupid, throw the Gondry line back at them.)

**B. Rogers' 5 ways to get inspired**
1. **Read something you normally wouldn't.**
   - At a roundtable, Will Wright said his inspiration came from Japanese gardening, architecture and biology. Rogers asked what about "normal" people who love comics, sci-fi films and games, and realised the answer while asking: that's exactly the problem.
   - Many developers love the *same* things (games, comics, movies). When everyone draws from the same well, games start to feel the same and **derivative**:
     - a hit movie's themes start showing up in games;
     - a hit game's mechanics get copied into other games;
     - you get eerie "synchronicity" where studios ship near-identical games at the same time (footnote: like the two volcano disaster movies, *Dante's Peak* and *Volcano*, both from 1997).
   - Fix: widen your inputs a little. No degree needed: flip through a magazine, spend an afternoon in a library, research a new topic online. Break the fanboy cycle.
2. **Take a walk, a drive or a shower.**
   - When a familiar routine keeps your conscious brain busy, your **subconscious** is free to wander and connect things it normally wouldn't. Those odd connections become ideas.
   - Practical safety: if ideas hit while driving, use a hands-free recorder or pull over before writing anything. (Also, Rogers notes, some designers could use the shower anyway.)
3. **Attend a lecture.**
   - Rogers fills notebooks at the **Game Developers Conference (GDC)** talks and discussions.
   - Share your ideas with other designers to "stretch the idea's legs": see where it bends and where it breaks. Be ready to hear that it's stupid (see the Gondry comeback).
4. **Play a game, preferably a bad one.**
   - Good games are useful, but bad games teach more: notice what was done poorly, then work out how you would fix it.
   - Analogy: lots of people "invented" the airplane before the Wright Brothers flew. Ideas often need several iterations, by several people, before they work.
   - Footnote: "good" is subjective: it can mean highly rated, best-selling, competently made, or simply cool.
5. **Regardless of all the above, follow your passion.**
   - Anything you love may end up in a design someday, and that love shows in the final game, even if what you love is comics and games.
   - Satoshi Tajiri turned his childhood love of **collecting insects** into *Pokémon*.
   - David Jaffe turned his love of **Ray Harryhausen** monster movies into *God of War*.
   - Shigeru Miyamoto regularly turns his real-world hobbies into game designs.
   - Bonus: following your passion makes design work feel less like work.

**C. A good idea vs a marketable idea**
- Having a good idea is different from having a **marketable** one.
- Marketing people have often told Rogers his concept was a **"designer's idea"**: something *he* would love to play but the general public wouldn't buy.
- He's torn:
  - **For the suits:** a game that sells lets you make more games.
  - **Against:** lots of innovative games were probably called too weird or unmarketable at some point. If everyone had listened, *PaRappa the Rapper*, *The Sims* and *Braid* would never exist.

**D. "Innovation" is mostly iteration**
- Rogers' own line, and the one direct quote in these notes: "If you think something is innovative, it just means you haven't been paying attention." (Scott Rogers)
- A totally original idea may still be out there somewhere, but most gameplay design works by **each game building on its predecessors**, and Rogers calls this one of the keys to great design.
- Even famously innovative games had ancestors:

| "Innovative" game | Its predecessor |
|---|---|
| *PaRappa the Rapper* (rhythm call-and-response) | *Simon* (repeat-the-pattern toy) |
| *The Sims* | *SimCity* |
| *Braid* (time rewinding) | *Blinx: The Time Sweeper* |

- Footnote on **Will Wright**: his whole career looks like the iteration of *one* idea, the world-building simulation: *SimCity → SimAnt → SimEarth → SimCity 2000 → The Sims → Spore*, scaling all the way up to galaxies. Getting to polish one idea for that long is a luxury most designers would envy.
- Rogers admits even this point isn't original: **Raph Koster** mapped the evolution of the shoot-'em-up in *A Theory of Fun for Game Design* (2004). Rogers does the same for platformers.

**E. The evolution of the platformer (book timeline + chart)**

| Game (year) | What it added to the genre |
|---|---|
| *Space Panic* (1980) | Walking hero who climbs ladders and digs holes to briefly trap/stun enemies |
| *Donkey Kong* (1981) | **Jumping**, plus a power-up (hammer) that can defeat enemies |
| *Popeye* (1982) | **Moving collectables** and environment mechanics the player can interact with |
| *Pitfall!* (1982) | Alternate moves: **vine swinging**, hopping across alligator heads |
| *Mario Bros.* (1983) | **Second player**; enemies beaten by **skill**, not just a power-up |
| *Pac-Land* (1984) | A **world map**, varied themed levels, dynamic hazards |
| *Ghosts 'n Goblins* (1985) | **Multiple weapons** incl. projectiles, **health** as armor that breaks off, fightable **bosses** |
| *Super Mario Bros.* (1985) | Tight controls, whimsical worlds, creative level design → spawned a wave of imitators |
| *Dark Castle* (1986) | Hero (Duncan) can **hide**; first game where falling didn't kill you outright, it dropped you into the dungeon |
| *Mega Man* (1987) | **Themed stages ending in themed bosses** whose powers you **take** after beating them |
| *Crash Bandicoot* (1996) | 3-D models and environments used to create the camera view called "**2.5-D**" |
| *Super Mario 64* (1996) | All of Mario's platforming moved into **true 3-D** |

- Who made them (as credited in the book): *Space Panic* Universal; *Donkey Kong*, *Popeye*, *Mario Bros.*, *Super Mario Bros.*, *Mario 64* Nintendo; *Pitfall!* Activision; *Pac-Land* Namco; *Ghosts 'n Goblins* and *Mega Man* Capcom; *Dark Castle* Silicon Beach Software; *Crash Bandicoot* Universal. Different companies kept borrowing from each other: the genre grew across studios, not inside one.

The book's hand-drawn **"Evolution of Platformer" chart** (credited "after Raph Koster") turns this into a family tree of bubbles and arrows:

```
Space Panic ─┬─> Popeye ─> Pitfall! ─> Dark Castle ─> [modern 2D single-screen:
             │     ^                                   Prince of Persia, Out of This World]
             └─> Donkey Kong ─┬─> Mario Bros. ─> Pac-Land ─> Super Mario Bros. ─┐
                              │                                                 │
                              └─> Ghosts 'n Goblins ─> Mega Man ──┐             │
                                                                  │             │
   Super Mario Bros. ─> [modern scrolling 2D: Sonic, Aero the Acrobat] ─> Crash Bandicoot
                                              Crash ─> [2.5D: Klonoa, Pandemonium!]
   Super Mario Bros. ─> Mario 64 ─┬─> [modern 3D platformer: Sly Cooper, Spyro]
                                  └─> [modern 3D action platformer: Ratchet & Clank, Maximo]
   Mega Man ──────────────────────────> [modern 3D action platformer]
```
- The point: **every idea builds on the one before**; each designer inspires the next. Rogers caps it with the famous "good artists steal" line attributed to Picasso. Translation: don't photocopy, *absorb* and transform.

**🎮 Book examples:** *Pac-Man*, *Mario*, *Katamari* → dumb-sounding pitches, huge hits · Will Wright → inspiration from gardening/architecture/biology; a career iterating one idea · *Pokémon* (bug collecting), *God of War* (Harryhausen), Miyamoto (hobbies) → passion fuels design · *PaRappa*, *The Sims*, *Braid* → "unmarketable" ideas that worked, *and* each had a predecessor · Platformer timeline → genres evolve one added mechanic at a time.

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- **Stupid-sounding F2P pitches that won:** "Pull pins to save a guy from lava" (a whole wave of pin-pull puzzle ads), "Roll dice on a Monopoly board, then raid your friends' landmarks" (*Monopoly GO!*), "A butler fixes up a mansion by matching tiles" (*Homescapes*), "A bird flaps between pipes and you die constantly" (*Flappy Bird*). Say it out loud and it sounds silly. That's normal.
- **Same-well problem is WORSE in mobile.** Everyone studies the same top-grossing charts, so the store fills with clones. Inputs from outside games (board games, sports, cooking, gardening, TikTok trends, real-world hobbies) are your edge.
- **Remix is the F2P default.** A simplified (not exhaustive) match-3 family tree, in the spirit of Rogers' platformer chart:
  - *Bejeweled* → popularized swap-to-match on PC/web.
  - *Candy Crush Saga* → level-by-level saga map, per-level goals, lives, boosters: match-3 as a long progression.
  - *Toon Blast* → swaps out swapping for **tap-to-blast** groups of blocks.
  - *Gardenscapes / Homescapes* → bolt a **renovation + story meta** on top: earn stars in levels, spend them to fix up a place.
  - *Royal Match* → takes the level + decorate formula and pushes speed, polish and juicy feedback.
  - Each step = one or two added ideas, exactly like the platformer chart.
- **Passion still matters in a metrics business.** Teams that actually love a genre notice the small feel details (cascade timing, booster satisfaction) that spreadsheets miss.
- **"Designer's idea" vs marketable** in F2P = "Will this get cheap installs and monetize?" Marketing's worry is legit (CPI, ad creatives, LTV). The fix isn't to give up: it's to test the weird idea cheaply (see Section 6).

**🪝 Make it stick:** Ideas are like LEGO bricks. Nobody invents a new brick; the masterpiece is in how you snap old ones together. Five ways to find bricks: **"Really Weird Llamas Play Piano"** = **R**ead odd stuff · **W**alk/shower · **L**ecture · **P**lay bad games · **P**assion.

> ⚠️ **2010 → 2026:** "Attend a lecture" now also means free GDC Vault talks, YouTube postmortems, deconstruction blogs and newsletters on mobile games, and Discord communities. Same principle (expose your idea to other designers) with way less airfare.

## 2. What Do Gamers Want?
**🧠 Big idea:** Players usually can't tell you what they want until they see it, so you need a passionate vision. Underneath it all they want one thing: **good games** that let them feel like someone they aren't, made for a clearly chosen audience.

**📋 The details:**
- **Players don't know what they want until it's shown to them** → designers need ideas born from **passion** and a clear **vision** of what the game should be.
- Players can *sense* when developers are passionate (Rogers: like a dog smelling fear).
- Hold on to your unique vision, but accept the final game probably won't turn out exactly as imagined.
- The simple answer, in shouty caps in the book (footnote: because it's a VERY IMPORTANT THING): **GAMERS WANT GOOD GAMES.**
  - There's no guarantee yours will be good. Nobody *tries* to make a bad game, yet bad games still ship, for many reasons the book covers later.
- **Wish fulfillment:** Tim Schafer (*Psychonauts*, *Brütal Legend*) says all good game characters are wish fulfillment: a chance to be something you're not in real life. Rogers extends it to games in general. Games should let players feel:
  - **powerful · smart · sneaky · successful · rich · bad · heroic**
- **Pick your audience early: casual or hardcore?**
  - The rise of **casual gaming** created a whole new market: players who don't want to grind for hours at a time.
  - Locking the audience down near the start lets you **rule out design decisions early** (saves arguments and wasted work).
- **Ask: what age is my audience?** Rogers, who has made dozens of kids' games, noticed:
  - **Kids always want the game made for kids older than them.** An 8-year-old wants the 10-year-old's game; a 10-year-old wants the 13-year-old's; a 13-year-old wants the game for 18-year-olds.
  - Kids often reject games aimed squarely at their age: "that's for my little brother" is the ultimate kid insult.
- **Don't talk down to kids.** Developers new to kids' games tend to oversimplify ("it can't be too hard, it's for kids"). Wrong: kids are smart, often better gamers than adults, and often pick up concepts faster.
- **Real kid limitations to respect:**
  1. Small hands can't handle overly complex control schemes.
  2. Kids in first grade or younger (about 6–7) may not read complex words or long text.
  3. Watch the swearing.

**🎮 Book examples:** Tim Schafer's wish-fulfillment idea · the "little brother" put-down → kids aspire upward · casual gaming boom → a market that doesn't want long grinds.

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- **"Good games" in F2P = good for *months*.** A good F2P game has a satisfying **core loop** in the first 30 seconds *and* a reason to come back tomorrow (meta, social, events). "Good" is measured: D1/D7/D30 retention, session frequency, reviews.
- **Wish fulfillment drives genres.** Power fantasy → *Survivor.io*, *Archero* (become an unstoppable one-person army). Rich/successful → *Monopoly GO!*, *Coin Master* (big-number jackpots). Smart → *Royal Match*, *Two Dots* ("I solved it"). Builder/boss → *Clash of Clans*, *Township* (run your own village/town). Heroic and collector → *AFK Journey*, *Genshin Impact*.
- **Casual vs hardcore became a spectrum:** hyper-casual (seconds to learn, ad-monetized) → hybrid-casual (simple core + light meta, ads + IAP) → casual (match-3, merge, sim) → mid-core (*Clash Royale*, *Brawl Stars*, *Marvel Snap*) → core (4X like *Whiteout Survival*, gacha RPGs). Pick a spot on purpose: it sets session length, monetization model, UA cost and art style.
- **The "older than me" rule = aspirational targeting.** Teens want what looks like it's for older players, and many adults like cartoon-styled games that still feel "grown-up" in depth. Casual mobile also skews to a broad adult audience. Know who actually plays.
- **Kids in mobile:** apps aimed at children fall under extra privacy and advertising rules (for example COPPA in the US and store "Kids" category policies). Aggressive monetization aimed at kids is an ethical and legal minefield. Design simple touch controls and icon-first UI for pre-readers.

**🪝 Make it stick:** Players are like diners who can't describe the dish they want, but they'll know in one bite. Cook with conviction, and never serve the kids' menu to a kid who wants the steak.

> ⚠️ **2010 → 2026:** In 2010 "casual" was a newcomer market. Today casual is mobile's largest audience by far, and "hardcore" mobile players exist too. You also have data: store reviews, analytics and playtests let you see what players want much sooner than the book's era. But data tells you what players do with what *exists*; it still doesn't invent the thing they didn't know they wanted. Vision still matters.

## 3. Why I Hate "Fun"
**🧠 Big idea:** Nobody can guarantee an idea will be fun, and after weeks of playtesting you lose the ability to judge. So instead of chasing "fun", start with a fun core and systematically remove everything that's **un-fun**.

**📋 The details:**
- "Is it fun?" is the question Rogers dreads most when developing ideas.
- Academics have tried to define fun. Designer **Marc LeBlanc** lists **8 kinds of fun** (from his GDC lecture and his 8kindsoffun website):

| Kind of fun | What it feels like |
|---|---|
| **Sensation** | Pleasure of the senses: sights, sounds, juicy feedback |
| **Fellowship** | Playing with/against other people, belonging |
| **Fantasy** | Being someone else, somewhere else (make-believe) |
| **Discovery** | Exploring, finding new stuff |
| **Narrative** | Drama and story unfolding |
| **Expression** | Self-expression, creating, customizing |
| **Challenge** | Overcoming obstacles, mastery |
| **Submission** | Pastime: zoning out in a comfortable, relaxing loop |

- Rogers finds the classification interesting but **not very useful in the field**, because:
  - an idea (or mechanic, or boss fight) can sound fun **on paper** but not be fun once built;
  - or it works, but it's only fun **to you**.
- **Fun is subjective**, like humor.
- **Fun decays with repetition:** something fun on play #1 is almost never fun on play #100. Developers replay the same level hundreds of times during production and **lose all objectivity**.
  - The book's two-panel cartoon: a producer asks "How's it going?" ("good"), then "So, is it fun yet?" The designer, mid-playtest: "I have no idea."
- Rogers' one truth about fun, also shouted in caps: **NOBODY CAN PROMISE THAT YOUR GAME IDEA WILL TURN OUT FUN.**
- You can stack the odds by copying proven gameplay, but that often produces a **clone** (think of all the mediocre FPS and survival horror games).
- **The Theory of Un-Fun:**
  1. Start with a fun idea.
  2. As you build, whenever you find something that isn't fun (**un-fun**), remove it.
  3. Once all the un-fun is gone, what's left should be the fun.
- Sounds obvious, yet many teams ship with bad mechanics, ugly art and broken cameras because they **got used to them** and stopped seeing them as problems (no objectivity left).
- ⚠️ Caveat: the core idea must be fun to begin with. Otherwise, after you've cut all the un-fun, nothing is left!
- **Apply it repeatedly** during development: stop, look at the game, **write a list** of what's making it un-fun. Typical suspects:
  - crappy camera · lousy controls · bad animations · too hard · too easy.
- A producer's advice to Rogers: **"Don't be too dear."** Don't be afraid to kill bad ideas (kill the un-fun). There are always more ideas.

**🎮 Book examples:** LeBlanc's 8 kinds of fun · the "is it fun yet?" producer cartoon · clone FPS/survival-horror glut → proven formulas don't guarantee fun.

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- **The 8 kinds of fun map straight onto F2P genres:**
  - Sensation → the cascade/explosion "juice" in *Royal Match* or *Candy Crush Saga*.
  - Fellowship → clans and clan wars in *Clash of Clans*; team play in *Brawl Stars*.
  - Fantasy → *Genshin Impact*'s world; being a mayor in *Township*.
  - Discovery → opening new areas/chains in *Merge Mansion*, *Travel Town*.
  - Narrative → the drama between levels in *Homescapes*.
  - Expression → decorating choices, skins, *Roblox* creation.
  - Challenge → hard levels, PvP ladders, *Marvel Snap* ranked.
  - Submission → *Block Blast!* or idle games as a soothing pastime.
- **F2P has a superpower Rogers didn't: analytics find the un-fun for you.** Funnel drop-offs (which FTUE step loses 20% of players?), level churn spikes (players quit at level 47), session-end reasons, rage-quit patterns and review keywords are all un-fun detectors. Then A/B test removing or softening the problem.
- **Fresh eyes on tap:** because the team loses objectivity, F2P studios run regular external playtests (new players, recorded sessions). The first 5 minutes from a stranger's view is the most valuable un-fun audit you can get.
- **Watch for monetization-born un-fun.** Pop-up offers that interrupt play, energy walls at the wrong moment and ad spam are classic un-fun that teams "get used to". Players don't.
- **Clone trap:** "proven" mobile mechanics reduce risk but crowd you into the most expensive UA market. Clone the *structure* if you must, but add one ingredient that's truly yours.

**🪝 Make it stick:** The Theory of Un-Fun is the sculptor's method: you don't add the statue, you chip away everything that isn't the statue. But if you started with a potato, chipping just gets you a smaller potato. Remember the 8 kinds of fun: **"Six Friendly Foxes Dance Near Every Crazy Squirrel"** = Sensation, Fellowship, Fantasy, Discovery, Narrative, Expression, Challenge, Submission.

> ⚠️ **2010 → 2026:** LeBlanc's 8 kinds come from the **MDA framework** (Mechanics–Dynamics–Aesthetics; Hunicke, LeBlanc & Zubek), where they're called "aesthetics". Modern F2P teams also use player-motivation models (see Section 6). Rogers' skepticism still holds: a taxonomy helps you *aim*; only playtesting tells you if you *hit*.

## 4. Brainstorming
**🧠 Big idea:** Brainstorming is a structured way to generate lots of ideas fast: the right kit, a no-judgment rule, a diverse group, and pushing every idea until it's absurd. Judging comes *later*.

**📋 The details:**

**A. The five things you need**
1. A working brain
2. Something to write with
3. A surface to write it on (paper, board)
4. A room or space to work in
5. Collaborators (ideally also with working brains)

**B. Ground rules (set them BEFORE you start)**
- **No such thing as a stupid or bad idea** at this stage. Say yes to everything.
- **Mix disciplines:** invite programmers, artists, testers, writers, not just designers. The more diverse the group, the better; people surprise you with what they bring.
- Footnote caveat: everyone invited should understand **how games get made**, or you'll burn time on unrealistic ideas.

**C. How to run it**
- Think about everything you want the game to be, and **write it all down**.
- **Free-associate**: follow an idea as far as it goes. **Milk it completely.** When you reach the ridiculous, squeeze once more, *then* let it go.
- The book shows a page of Rogers' own **"Kart Racing Brainstorm"** notes, split into two buckets:
  - **Speed-related:** e.g. going so fast you skim across water, leaping huge gaps, phasing through walls, leaving a fire trail, slow-motion "bullet-time", drafting in another kart's slipstream, spinning donuts to whip up a tornado, rocket boosts and team-wide boosts.
  - **Damage-related:** e.g. flame blasts from the exhaust or front, sticky or bouncing bombs dropped behind, homing shots fired forward, bashing rivals to knock parts off, leeching health or shields, stealing power-ups, a bomb you must shoot before it goes off, a charge-up force field that hurts everyone nearby.
- Notice the topics don't strictly relate to each other; they're just things a combat/driving game *might* have.
- Ideas **don't need to be original** yet. You're just **cataloging**. Originality and fun get judged later, as you design further.

**D. Recording tools**
- Rogers likes a **very big whiteboard**; Post-it notes or index cards work too. The tool doesn't matter.
- What matters: **record every idea.** Even rejects can be reused in a future game.

**E. The "box and manual" exercise**
- Imagine you're making the retail **box** and the **manual**:
  - What's the **cover image**?
  - What **bullet points** go on the back of the box?
  - How would you explain the game in a **black-and-white, 16-page manual**?
- These constraints force you to strip the idea to its **bare essentials**.
- The book provides a **back-of-box template** with these slots:
  1. **Catchy slogan** across the top
  2. **Exciting image #1, #2, #3** (screenshots)
  3. **Gameplay feature #1, #2, #3** (one short description beside each image)
  4. A block of **legal information**
  5. **Development team logo** and **publisher logo**
  6. **Rating** box (what is your game's rating?)
  7. **Platform** (what system is it for?)
  8. **Barcode**

**🎮 Book examples:** Rogers' kart-racing brainstorm page → quantity first, sorted loosely by theme · back-of-box template → constraint forces clarity.

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- **The modern back of the box is your store page.** Same constraint trick, new format. Try the **F2P store-page template**:

| Box slot (2010) | F2P store-page equivalent (2026) |
|---|---|
| Catchy slogan | **App title + subtitle** (both short, character-limited on the stores) |
| Cover image | **App icon**: must read at thumb size next to 50 competitors |
| Exciting images #1–3 | **First 3 screenshots** (most users never scroll further) |
| Gameplay features #1–3 | **Screenshot captions**: one benefit each ("Relax with 1000s of levels") |
| 16-page manual | **FTUE**: can a new player get it in the first 60 seconds with almost no text? |
| Rating box | **Age rating** + "contains ads / in-app purchases" labels |
| Platform | iOS / Android (and maybe PC/web) |
| (new slot) | **15–30 s video ad**: the hook lives in the first 3 seconds |

- **Brainstorm buckets for F2P:** core-loop verbs, meta/progression layers, social features, LiveOps event ideas, monetization touchpoints (that feel *fair*), and "ad hook" moments that look great in a 3-second clip.
- **Mix disciplines = include UA/marketing and data people.** They know which ad hooks are currently cheap, and analysts know where current players churn. (But keep the no-judgment rule until the culling phase.)
- **Record everything = keep an idea backlog.** In F2P, today's rejected mechanic is next season's LiveOps event or mini-game.

**🪝 Make it stick:** Brainstorm kit = **"a Brain plus 4 Ps"**: **B**rain, **P**en, **P**aper, **P**lace, **P**eople. Rule: squeeze the lemon until it's ridiculous… then squeeze once more.

> ⚠️ **2010 → 2026:** Boxes and paper manuals are basically gone (especially on mobile). Keep the *principle*: describe your game in a tiny, fixed format before you build it. Today that's a store page, an ad storyboard, or a one-sentence hook.

## 5. Breaking Writer's Block (and then culling)
**🧠 Big idea:** Everyone gets stuck. Rogers gives five unblocking tricks, then the second half of ideation: cull ruthlessly and get an outsider's honest opinion.

**📋 The details:**

**A. Five tricks for writer's block** (no shame: it happens to everyone)
1. **Narrow your focus.** You may be juggling too much. Tackle problems one at a time: make an outline, break things down to tiny pieces if needed. Give each piece a deadline measured in **hours, not days**.
2. **Take a walk or exercise.** The brain runs on blood flow: get up and move instead of letting it pool in your backside. Ideas return once circulation does.
3. **Deal with whatever is distracting you.** Sometimes you're stuck because something else is nagging you (an unfiled expense report, a floor to vacuum). Handle it; once it's done it stops occupying your head.
4. **Jump ahead to the good stuff.** If a boring part (say, UI design) is bogging you down, work on an exciting part (a boss fight) for a while.
   - ⚠️ **Last resort only; it's dangerous.** Games run on schedules, deadlines and budgets. If you always pick the fun work over the drudge work and miss deadlines, the game, the team and even the company can suffer. **Don't procrastinate; manage your time responsibly.**
5. **Change your environment.** Offices are full of distractions: e-mail, games begging to be played, design docs waving their pages at you. Move to a conference room, or sit outside in the sun for some "vitamin Duh".

**B. Get critical: cull the list**
- Once you have a list, **narrow it down**. Some ideas jump out as **keepers**, others are clear **losers**.
- **Be merciless.** Better to have more good ideas than you can use than a game stuffed with bad ones.

**C. Show it to someone with no stake**
- **Miyamoto's "wife-o-meter":** he shows ideas to his wife; if she hates them, they're gone.
- Rogers used to show ideas to his office's **executive assistants**.
- Why: someone with **no creative investment** in the idea gives the clearest, most honest feedback.

**🎮 Book examples:** boss fight vs UI work → the jump-ahead temptation · Miyamoto's wife-o-meter → outsider filter · Rogers' executive-assistant test.

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- **Narrow focus = scope one loop at a time.** Don't design "the whole game"; design the next 30 seconds (core), then the next 30 minutes (session goals), then the next 30 days (meta/LiveOps). Timebox each in hours.
- **Jump-ahead danger in F2P:** the "boring" parts (FTUE, store UI, settings, loading flow, crash-free performance on low-end Android phones) often decide retention. Skipping to cool event design while the FTUE leaks players is a classic studio mistake.
- **The modern wife-o-meter:**
  - **Stranger playtests** (people who've never seen the game; watch silently).
  - **Ad creative tests:** run a short mock video of the concept and see if strangers actually click and install (more in Section 6).
  - **Market reality check:** do similar games exist in the top charts? Is that a sign of demand or of a saturated slot?
- **Cull with numbers *and* gut.** Score ideas on: fits the core fantasy? fits the audience? cheap to test? ad-able? monetizable without being predatory? Then still trust the "it just feels right" signal for the final pick.

**🪝 Make it stick:** Stuck? **"Focus, Move, Clear, Skip⚠️, Swap"** = narrow Focus · Move your body · Clear the distraction · Skip ahead (last resort!) · Swap location. Then run every idea through the wife-o-meter (or your own grandma-o-meter).

## 6. 📱 F2P Bonus: The Mobile Ideation Toolkit
*(Not in the 2010 book: this is the modern F2P layer on top of Rogers' principles.)*

**🧠 Big idea:** In F2P mobile, an idea is a bet on **who** plays, **why** they play, **where it fits in the market**, and **whether it survives a 3-minute session**. Validate those bets before you spend months building.

**📋 The details:**

**A. Player motivation: "why would anyone open this app?"**
- Rogers' wish-fulfillment list is a motivation model. Modern teams use richer ones:
  - **Quantic Foundry's gamer motivation model:** six clusters: **Action** (destruction, excitement), **Social** (competition, community), **Mastery** (challenge, strategy), **Achievement** (completion, power), **Immersion** (fantasy, story), **Creativity** (design, discovery).
  - **Self-Determination Theory:** people stay engaged when they feel **competence** (I'm getting better), **autonomy** (my choices matter) and **relatedness** (connection to others).
  - **Bartle's player types** (achievers, explorers, socializers, killers): older, from online worlds, still a handy shorthand.
- Use them to answer: *which 1–2 motivations is my game built around?* A match-3 hits mastery and completion; *Clash Royale* hits competition and strategy; *Hay Day* hits creativity and completion with a social layer.

**B. Market and genre gaps: the remix matrix**
- Rogers says most innovation builds on predecessors. In F2P that's a deliberate method:
  - **Proven core + fresh theme** (a known merge loop in a new setting).
  - **Proven core + new meta** (match-3 core + renovation story = the *Homescapes* model).
  - **Proven mechanic from another platform, re-shaped for touch** (survivor-like action reborn as one-thumb play in *Survivor.io*).
  - **Genre mash-up** (board game + social raiding in *Monopoly GO!*).
- Look for **gaps**: an audience that loves a genre but whose taste in theme isn't served; a mechanic popular on PC with no good touch version; a mega-genre where every leader feels the same.
- ⚠️ Beware "blue ocean" fantasies: a gap may exist because nobody wants it. Check that *similar* games prove the motivation exists.

**C. What mobile players want in short sessions**
- **Instant start:** in the loop within seconds of tapping the icon; no long intros, no mandatory reading.
- **Bite-sized sessions:** a meaningful goal you can finish in a few minutes (one level, one battle, one merge chain).
- **Interruption-tolerant:** auto-save always; a phone call or app switch costs nothing.
- **One-hand / portrait friendly** for many casual genres; readable with **sound off**.
- **Clear next goal:** always show the next thing to do (a star to earn, a chest to open, a task bar to fill).
- **"Just one more" hook:** near-miss, a reward almost ready, a streak to keep.
- **Respect for time and wallet:** fair energy systems, optional rewarded ads, no pay-to-stop-suffering traps. Ethical design is good long-term business (and loot-box regulation exists in some countries).

**D. Validate before you build**
1. **One-sentence hook test:** "It's X, but Y." If you can't say it in one line, players won't get it from an ad.
2. **Market scan:** list 5–10 closest competitors; note their core loop, meta, monetization, and what reviews complain about (their un-fun is your opportunity).
3. **Fake-door / creative test:** make a short video or static ads showing the concept and measure interest (click-through, cost per install). Ideas nobody clicks die here, before any code.
4. **Paper or greybox prototype** of the core loop in days, not months. Is the 30-second loop fun with ugly boxes? (The Theory of Un-Fun needs a fun core to carve.)
5. **Stranger playtest:** 5 new players, no help; watch where they get confused or bored.
6. **Soft launch** in a few test markets: check early retention (D1/D7), the FTUE funnel, session length and first monetization signals before going global.
- Rule of thumb: **spend the least money that can kill the idea.** Every stage is a wife-o-meter with numbers.

**🎮 Book link:** This is Rogers' own process (get inspired → brainstorm → cull → outsider test) with a data layer on top.

**🪝 Make it stick:** F2P ideas are like startups: most should die young and cheap. Your job isn't to protect your baby; it's to find out fast whether it can walk.

## 🏆 Universal Truths & Clever Ideas
1. **All gamers want are good games.**
   📱 so for F2P: "good" means a fun 30-second loop *and* a reason to return tomorrow, next week, next month.
2. **There's no guarantee your game will be fun.**
   📱 so for F2P: assume it isn't until prototypes, playtests and soft-launch numbers say otherwise.
3. **Start with a fun idea; as you build, cut the "un-fun". Whatever remains should be the fun.**
   📱 so for F2P: funnels, level churn and reviews point straight at the un-fun; fix or remove it, then re-measure.
4. **Don't be too precious with ideas. Throw out some *good* ones too if they don't fit or you have more than you need.**
   📱 so for F2P: a feature that doesn't serve the core fantasy or the target player adds complexity, dev cost and FTUE friction. Park it in the backlog for a future event.
5. **Ideas are cheap; what you do with them is what counts.**
   📱 so for F2P: execution (feel, pacing, economy, LiveOps cadence) is why one match-3 earns millions and its clone earns nothing.
6. **If you're stuck, take a break, but don't procrastinate.**
   📱 so for F2P: LiveOps calendars don't wait; timebox your thinking.
