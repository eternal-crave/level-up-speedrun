## ⚡ TL;DR — the 30-second version
- **A pitch is your GDD with the "twiddley bits" stripped out** — only what's great and original, packed into a few readable slides. Nobody in the room cares about your lore yet; they care how the game *plays* (and earns).
- **Pitch equation: demo > design doc > presentation > pitch.** A playable thing beats any amount of talking. (F2P version: a build with real retention numbers beats every deck on Earth.)
- **Video games are made by people.** Most games that go badly were *planned* badly: schedule for sickness, vacations, broken hardware, scope creep, late changes, and your own design blinders.
- **Pick a production style and commit:** a **vertical slice** (one chunk at ~80% final quality) or a **horizontal layer** (the whole game "up" in gray box first). Either way: don't hide behind "emergent gameplay" — good design is planned, not stumbled into.
- **Sequels are a chance to get it right:** keep the spine, fix what was broken, use the **30/70** rule (30% new, 70% proven), don't betray what fans loved, and add fresh stuff — but not *too* fresh.
- **Book's last words:** rest, be fair and generous, keep playing (even bad games), enjoy the ride — and remember game-making is *exactly like making chili* (recipe, ingredients, boil, simmer, season to taste).

## 1. And Now the Hard Part — what a pitch is
**🧠 Big idea:** Finishing the design is not finishing the game. Before anyone *makes* it, someone has to *fund and publish* it — and to get a publisher you need a **pitch**: a streamlined, easy-to-swallow version of your GDD.

**📋 The details:**
- **Where you are now (per the book):** you've got a great game idea and a 200+ page GDD. Rogers' verdict: you're not done — your work is *just beginning*.
  - Order of operations: **pitch → find a publisher → publisher funds/publishes → you actually make the game.**
- **Pitch (definition):** a slimmed-down, digestible version of the game design document that keeps *everything great and original* about the game and drops the **"twiddley bits."**
  - 📝 **Twiddley bits** (industry slang, from the footnote): all the details you sweated over that other people don't need to hear when you describe your game. They matter for *making* the game, not for *selling* it to a publisher.
- **Format:** most pitches happen in boardrooms in front of groups → Rogers strongly recommends **presentation software** (PowerPoint or similar) for the pitch document.
- A full slide-by-slide outline lives in **Bonus Level 9** (High Concept Pitch Presentation) → see BL9 notes.
- **The pitch skeleton (5 basics):**
  1. **Title screen with logo**
  2. **Company profile** (who you are, why you can pull this off)
  3. **High concept** (the one-breath version of the game)
  4. **A few pages of details** (a few — not 40)
  5. **Proof** of why the game will be awesome and/or make everyone a lot of money
- **Slide-making basics (don't skip these, they're where people faceplant):**
  - Pick a **font you can actually read**.
  - **Don't cram** too much information onto one slide.
  - **Everyone loves pictures.** Show, don't paragraph.

**🎮 Book examples:** No specific games here — the point is the *document*: your 200-page GDD becomes a 10–15 slide sales tool.

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- In F2P, "the publisher" might be: an **internal greenlight committee** at a studio, an external **mobile publisher** (they often fund/run user acquisition), **investors**, or a **product lead** you're convincing to put your feature on the roadmap. Same skill, different room.
- The F2P version of "proof it'll make money" isn't vibes — it's **market fit** (are similar games earning?), a **monetization hypothesis** (what will players buy and *why*?), and **KPI targets** (what D1/D7 retention and ARPDAU you expect, and what numbers would make you kill it). Full template in §14.
- Twiddley bits in F2P: the exact drop rates of your 47 chest types, the backstory of every hero, the full event calendar. Keep them in the GDD; put the **core loop diagram** on the slide.

**🪝 Make it stick:** Your GDD is the whole cow. The pitch is the steak on a nice plate. Nobody at the dinner table wants to see the rest of the cow. 🐄➡️🥩

## 2. No One Cares About Your Stupid Little World
**🧠 Big idea:** A pitch is like an **artist's portfolio** — show your best work, but not so much that you bury the audience. And *especially* don't drown them in lore: at pitch stage, nobody cares about your world's politics; they want to know **how the game plays**.

**📋 The details:**
- **Portfolio analogy:** curate. Best pieces only. Too many pieces = overwhelmed audience.
- **The story-pitch horror story:** Rogers sat through a pitch where the writer launched into the intricate workings of his fantasy universe. Rogers tuned out almost immediately — too much to process at once.
  - He didn't care who the **Nebulons** were or what a **Galactic Imperium** meant. He just wanted to know **how the game played**.
- **The nuance:** inventing a world *is* fun, and world details *do* matter for making a world feel real. But **at the pitch stage, nobody else cares** about those details.
- **Rule:** don't overburden your audience with story. **Keep it simple** — and keep those details to yourself... for now. (They come out later, in production, where they belong.)
- 🖼️ **Book cartoon (p. 418):** an eager storyteller recites a galactic history lesson (a far-future year, a "Hegemony" muscling in on an "Armada" at some star cluster, the balance of the imperium shifting for the first time in a millennium...) while the listener slumps behind his desk, eyes glazed. Dual-code it: *the more proper nouns per sentence, the faster the publisher's soul leaves their body.*

**🎮 Book examples:** The anonymous fantasy-world story pitch (Nebulons + Galactic Imperium) → shows lore-first pitching kills attention.

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- F2P greenlight rooms care about, in roughly this order: **Who is this for? What do they do every 30 seconds (core gameplay), every session (core loop), every week (meta/LiveOps)? Why do they come back? Why do they pay? What's the market evidence?** Lore is maybe slide 12, if at all.
- Even huge narrative gacha games are *pitched* on their hook (e.g., "open-world action RPG with elemental reactions and a party-switching combat system") — not on the 400-year history of a fictional nation.
- In your F2P store page, the same rule holds: the first screenshot shows **gameplay**, not a wall of story text.

**🪝 Make it stick:** Picture a publisher with a "LORE METER" on their forehead. Every proper noun fills it 10%. At 100% their head becomes a loading spinner. 🌀 Say "Nebulon" fewer times.

> ⚠️ **2010 → 2026:** Still 100% true — arguably more so. Mobile execs see dozens of concepts; market-fit data and a playable prototype matter far more than worldbuilding. Narrative can be a *differentiator* in F2P (it helps retention in some genres), but you pitch it as a *feature with a purpose* ("story chapters unlock weekly to drive D7"), not as a history lecture.

## 3. The six rules of thumb for pitching
**🧠 Big idea:** Beyond "cut the lore," Rogers gives six practical rules: **pitch to the right people, in the right place, well rehearsed, knowing your game cold, focused on one idea — and pitch even if all you have is great tech.**

**📋 The details:**

### Rule 1 — Pitch to the right people
- Just like when you wrote your game outline (→ L04 one-sheet; audience thinking from L02), think about **who you're selling to**.
- Rogers has often heard, after a pitch: *great pitch... but we'd never publish that kind of game.* Sad — because a little **research about the publisher** would have avoided it. You waste your time *and* theirs.
- **Some publishers only publish certain genres.** Don't pitch a **family game** to a publisher specializing in **hardcore action**.
- **But:** many publishers publish a wide range, and **company objectives change**. **Timing is everything** — you might walk in the very week they decide they want, say, an RTS about cybernetic death-mechs piloted by farm animals. (Or, y'know, maybe not. 🐄🤖)

### Rule 2 — Pitch in a controlled environment
- You can't always choose where a pitch happens, but aim for a place where **the audience's attention is on you and your presentation**.
- Book contrast: pitching on the **noisy show floor of E3 = bad**. Pitching in a **private meeting room at E3 = much better**.

### Rule 3 — Be prepared
- A pitch is a **performance → practice it.** Rehearse in front of your peers and ask for feedback on **both content and delivery**.
- Don't be afraid to tap your **inner actor**: you're telling the story of your game, and you want the publisher as excited about it as you are.
- **Let the best speaker present.** If a teammate is better in front of a group, have them give the pitch — *under your supervision*. You still attend, to **answer questions and fill in details**.
- **You only get one chance to impress** — make the game shine in the best possible light.
- **Tech checklist from the book:**
  - Make sure your **computer works with any kind of setup** (projectors, ports, etc.).
  - Always bring **backup data** (2010: thumb drive or disc).
  - Rogers has seen presentations **stop dead because the sound didn't work**.
  - Bring **plenty of printed copies** of documents — tricky because the **number of attendees is unpredictable**.
  - The ultimate test: **could you still pitch if the power went out?**
  - Motto: like the Boy Scouts — **be prepared**.
- 🖼️ **The "pitch kit" figure (p. 419)** — a smiling presenter in front of a projected "Dude With Sword — Pitch Presentation" slide, labeled with everything to bring/do:
  - 👀 **Make eye contact** while speaking
  - 😁 **Be enthusiastic and smile!**
  - 👔 **Dress accordingly** (match the room)
  - 🪪 **Business cards**
  - 📄 **Take-away documentation** (something they keep after you leave)
  - 🎮 **Playable demo** (controller on the table)
  - 💻 **Laptop computer**
  - 💾 **Flash or backup drive**
  - 📽️ **PowerPoint or video** (projectable)

### Rule 4 — Know your project inside and out (and then some)
- Even though the game doesn't exist yet, **know everything you possibly can about it**.
- Publishers **ask questions to poke holes** in your design. Not out of malice — they're hunting for **topics that could cause trouble once production starts**.
- Be able to go **in depth**, but **don't ramble** — don't drown people in details unless they ask.
- **Don't know an answer?** Turn it into a conversation: tell them you'd like to hear *their* thoughts on it. (Honest, collaborative, and buys you time.)

### Rule 5 — Keep the focus
- Some teams bring **several pitches** to find out what the publisher likes. Rogers isn't a fan. More pitches means:
  - **(a)** the meeting runs longer,
  - **(b)** less time is spent on each game,
  - **(c)** you may not get **consensus** on which idea is best.
- There's always **one idea that stands out** — so why muddy the waters? Let attendees concentrate on that one. You can **come back another time** to pitch another idea.
- If you *do* present several, make sure **all are at the same level of quality** (one weak pitch drags down the others).

### Rule 6 — Pitch to represent
- Even if you **lack a killer pitch or a truly original idea**, standout **technology** or a working **prototype of a gameplay mechanic** may still be worth showing a publisher.
- Why: sometimes a publisher **owns a license but hasn't picked a studio** to turn it into a game. Your tech/prototype could make you that studio.
- **Timing and luck** play as big a role in pitching as a great idea and strong presentation.

**🎮 Book examples:**
- E3 show floor vs. private meeting room → controlled environment.
- "Family game to a hardcore-action publisher" → pitching to the wrong people.
- The farm-animal death-mech RTS → timing/luck can make a weird pitch land (or not).
- "Dude With Sword" (the book's running sample game) → used for the pitch-kit illustration.

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
| Book rule | F2P version |
|---|---|
| Right people | Study the publisher's/studio's **portfolio and revenue mix**. A hyper-casual publisher wants cheap-to-make, high-CPI-test-score toys; a midcore strategy house wants deep economies and long LTV. Don't pitch a 4X to a hyper-casual shop. |
| Controlled environment | Today a lot of pitching is a **video call** → control *that* environment: stable connection, quiet room, screen-share tested, slides that read at small sizes. |
| Be prepared | Have a **backup video capture** of the prototype in case the build crashes or the device won't mirror. Test on the actual device you'll present on. |
| Know it inside out | Expect **economy and KPI questions**: "What's your D1 target? How does a non-spender progress? What's the main sink? What's the CPI risk for this art style?" |
| Keep the focus | One concept per meeting. If a studio wants options, send a short **concept slate** later — at equal polish. |
| Pitch to represent | A great **tech demo or mechanic prototype** can win you a work-for-hire or co-dev deal, or a licensed-IP mobile project, even without an original concept. |

**🪝 Make it stick — the 6 Rs of pitching:** **R**ight people · **R**ight room · **R**ehearse · **R**ock-solid answers · **R**estrict to one idea · **R**epresent (even with just tech). Six Rs, zero lore.

> ⚠️ **2010 → 2026:** E3 is gone (it was officially ended in 2023); pitches now happen at events like GDC and Gamescom, at mobile-focused conferences, in publisher portals, and over video calls. "Bring a disc" becomes "have the build on the device *and* a cloud link *and* an offline video." The principle — control the environment and plan for tech failure — is unchanged.

## 4. The pitch equation & the demo dilemma
**🧠 Big idea:** After 5+ years at a publisher hearing scores of pitches, Rogers ranks what sells a game: **GAME DEMO > GAME DESIGN DOCUMENT > PRESENTATION > PITCH.** Something playable beats a document, which beats slides, which beats just talking.

**📋 The details:**
- **The pitch equation** (in the Universal Truths it's phrased as demo > GDD > pitch presentation > pitch outline):
  1. **Game demo** — strongest: they can *play* it.
  2. **Game design document** — proves you've thought it through.
  3. **Presentation** — slides and visuals.
  4. **Pitch** (verbal pitch / outline) — weakest on its own.
- **The demo dilemma:**
  - ❌ **Demos are a lot of work.** If not planned for, they **steal time from production** of the main game and **disrupt the flow** of the project.
  - ❌ They can trigger **crunch time** — long hours trying to jam content into the game.
  - ✅ But demos **prove you're serious**, and they let potential publishers **get their hands on code** — which is sometimes *the* best way to sell a game.
- So: **plan for the demo in your schedule** instead of treating it as a surprise side-quest.

**🎮 Book examples:** Rogers' own experience as a publisher-side reviewer of many pitches → the source of the equation.

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- F2P's pitch equation is even steeper: **live metrics > playable prototype > GDD > deck > idea.**
  - A prototype with **real retention data** from a small test (or a soft launch) is the ultimate "demo."
  - Hyper-casual publishers popularized an even earlier proof: test **marketability** (cost per install from a short gameplay video or ultra-minimal build) *before* building much of anything.
- The demo dilemma also exists in F2P: building a shiny **pitch build** or **vertical slice** for a publisher can stall core-loop iteration. Budget it in the plan.

**🪝 Make it stick:** **"Demos Get Publishers Paying"** → **D**emo > **G**DD > **P**resentation > **P**itch. Or: *Talking is a trailer. Slides are a poster. A demo is a free sample at the food court — that's the one people actually buy from.* 🍗

## 5. Video Games Is a Haaaard Business — why good games still flop
**🧠 Big idea:** Making games sounds like fun and games, but it's brutal work — and even great work can fail for reasons outside your control. The one thing you *can* control is planning, and Rogers' bolded "very important thing" is the anchor for all of it: **video games are made by people.**

**📋 The details:**
- **It's hard work:** lots of effort and lots of hours to design and build a game.
- **"Sure hits" can flop:** even after all that work, a game that looks like a guaranteed success can get **poor reviews** or **mediocre sales**.
- **Great reviews ≠ great sales:** plenty of well-made, fun, well-reviewed games still **sold badly**.
- **Publisher-side reasons a game can underperform:**
  - The publisher **can't afford to promote** the game as much as you think it deserves.
  - **Sales projections** get set at levels the game simply **can't reach**.
- **Team-side reasons (it's not *all* the publisher's fault):**
  - Some team members **don't put their best work** into the game.
  - **Family and health issues** distract people from their jobs.
  - Worst case: your **lead designer or another key person gets seriously ill or dies** during production.
- 🔑 **The very important thing:** "VIDEO GAMES ARE MADE BY PEOPLE" (Rogers). People get sick, burn out, have babies, go on vacation, and have bad weeks. These things *will* happen.
- **Therefore:** give your game a fighting chance. **Most games that go badly are the result of bad planning** → which leads to the troubleshooting list in §6.

**🎮 Book examples:** None named — Rogers deliberately keeps the flop examples anonymous; the point is that quality alone doesn't guarantee success.

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- F2P has its own "great game, bad sales" trap: a fun game whose **CPI is higher than its LTV** (it costs more to acquire a player than they'll ever spend) is commercially dead no matter how good the reviews are.
- "Publisher can't fund promotion" = **user-acquisition (UA) budget** limits. In F2P, marketing spend *is* the launch.
- "Sales projections set too high" = an unrealistic **business case** (e.g., assuming top-grossing-chart retention for a brand-new IP). Overpromised KPIs get projects killed.
- "Made by people" matters double in **LiveOps**: the game never ships "once." If your plan requires the same three people to make a new event every week forever, the plan is broken.

**🪝 Make it stick:** Your Gantt chart is not a list of robots. Every bar on it has a human inside who sometimes gets the flu. 🤒 Plan like it.

## 6. The production troubleshooting list
**🧠 Big idea:** Rogers' checklist for keeping production alive: **plan for people and hardware problems, don't overpromise, lock the design early, break your own game, respect cultural differences, get outside eyes, and cut what doesn't work.**

**📋 The details:**
1. **Plan ahead.**
   - Prepare for **team illnesses and vacations**.
   - Prepare for **hardware failures, brown-outs (power dips), and other technical problems**.
2. **Be smart when creating your design.**
   - **Don't overpromise content.** Don't bite off more than the team can chew.
3. **Lock down design and content as early as possible.**
   - Constantly changing content in pursuit of perfection **wears the team down** and **may even kill the game**.
4. **Play the game the way players will — and break it.**
   - Players **will always find ways to break your game**.
   - Don't only play it the way it's "meant to be played." **Do weird things**, bang on it until it breaks — **then fix it and keep going**.
5. **Respect cultural differences in international versions.**
   - Example: for the **Japan** release of ***Fat Princess*** (SCE, 2009), developer **Titan Studios** had to **give its cartoony four-fingered cast a fifth finger**.
   - 📝 Footnote — Rogers gives at least **three explanations** for why a four-fingered hand is taboo in Japan:
     - **Yubitsume:** a Yakuza practice of cutting off the top of the little finger at the knuckle as atonement → the maimed hand looks four-fingered.
     - **The number four** is pronounced *shi*, which also means **death** — an unlucky number, much like 13 in Western cultures.
     - **Burakumin:** a social class that still faces discrimination. Holding up four fingers was a way to label them (their jobs often involved four-legged animals), so it turned into a **slur**; today's Buraku activist groups have **taken legal action over four-finger imagery** in Japanese media.
6. **Remove your design blinders.**
   - **Design blinders** = what happens when you're **too close to your game**: creators start believing the game is **too easy**, or that **half-baked elements are "good enough."**
   - Fixes: **recruit other people to play** and give feedback; use **focus group testing** where it makes sense; **iterate at every stage** of production.
   - Publishers are very good at this — providing what Rogers calls the **"10,000 foot view"**: an **objective look** at your game that spots **weak spots** and helps **strengthen the good parts**.
7. **If something doesn't work, throw it out.**
   - **Don't be precious** about your ideas — they're a **dime a dozen**.
   - When you throw something out, **have a backup plan** in mind.
   - But **don't make throwing work away a habit** — it's better to **plan ahead and anticipate problems** than to waste work.
   - If you *are* tossing good ideas, that's a good sign: you have **too many** good ideas! **Save them for the sequel.**
   - 📝 Footnote: **S.I.F.S. — "save it for the sequel"** — was a common saying at studios Rogers worked at.

**🎮 Book examples:**
- ***Fat Princess*** (Titan Studios / SCE, 2009) → four-fingered characters redrawn with five fingers for Japan = localization is more than translation.
- Publishers' "10,000 foot view" → outside perspective cures design blinders.

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- **Plan ahead:** F2P studios plan **LiveOps calendars** months out, build **event templates** you can re-skin, and keep a **content buffer** (finished events in the bank) so one sick artist doesn't cancel next week's event.
- **Don't overpromise:** the classic F2P overpromise is a soft-launch build with three metas, PvP, guilds and a battle pass, none of them finished. Ship the **core loop + one meta** and prove retention first.
- **Lock early:** lock the **core loop** and **economy structure** before you scale content production; changing the currency model after 300 levels are built is a nightmare.
- **Break it like players:** F2P players hunt **exploits** — changing the device clock to skip timers, refund abuse, duplicate-reward bugs. Exploits spread in hours on Reddit, Discord and YouTube. **QA your economy**, not just your levels.
- **Cultural differences — global launch edition:**
  - **Localization** of text, symbols, colors, gestures and holidays (your Halloween event isn't universal).
  - **Regulation:** Japan banned "kompu gacha" (complete-the-set gacha) in 2012; China and South Korea require **publishing gacha/loot-box odds**; **Belgium** treats paid loot boxes as gambling. Some markets require content changes (e.g., gore, gambling imagery) for approval.
- **Design blinders → F2P tools:** remote **playtests** with fresh players, **analytics funnels** (where exactly do people drop in the FTUE?), and **A/B tests**. Data is the modern "10,000 foot view" — it doesn't care how much you love your feature.
- **Throw it out:** F2P teams cut features that don't move **retention or monetization KPIs**. And S.I.F.S. becomes **"save it for a LiveOps update"** — your cut mini-game can return later as a limited-time event mode.

**🪝 Make it stick — be a PLANNER:**
- **P**lan for people & hardware problems
- **L**ock design and content early
- **A**void overpromising
- **N**uke-test it like a player (break it!)
- **N**ations differ — check culture
- **E**yes from outside (remove blinders)
- **R**emove what doesn't work (S.I.F.S.)

> ⚠️ **2010 → 2026:** Focus groups and the publisher's eye still exist, but modern mobile teams get the "10,000 foot view" mostly from **telemetry and A/B tests** on real players. Also, "lock down design" softens in F2P: the game keeps changing after launch by design. What you lock is the **foundation** (core loop, economy architecture, tech), so that *content* can keep changing safely.

## 7. Cutting content the smart way
**🧠 Big idea:** Every game cuts content. The trick is **when** and **what**: cut as much as possible in **preproduction**, cut for the **right reasons**, and **never yank something wired into multiple systems.**

**📋 The details:**
- **Cutting content happens on every game.** It's normal, not failure.
- **Timing:** the more you cut during **preproduction**, the less you'll have to cut during **production** (when cuts are expensive and painful).
- **Motivation:** make sure you're cutting **for the right reasons** (it doesn't work, it's not fun, it's out of scope) — not on a whim.
- **Danger zone:** **don't cut or change content that connects to and affects multiple game systems** — that's how you create a cascade of bugs and design holes.

**🎮 Book examples:** None named.

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- High-risk F2P cuts: anything touching the **economy** (a currency, a sink, a reward source), **progression gates**, or **social systems**. Removing a currency sink can inflate the whole economy; removing a reward source can starve progression.
- Lower-risk cuts: a cosmetic set, a single event mode, a side mini-game.
- Rule of thumb: before cutting, draw the **dependency web** — which loops, rewards and UI screens reference this thing?

**🪝 Make it stick:** Cutting content is like Jenga: pull loose blocks early and the tower stands. Pull the block six others rest on, late, and everything crashes. 🧱

## 8. Vertical slice vs. horizontal layer
**🧠 Big idea:** Two ways to build a game: a **vertical slice** (one piece built to near-final quality, as a demo and template) or a **horizontal layer** (the *entire* game brought up at once in rough **gray box**, then polished). Rogers' truth: **pick one and go.**

**📋 The details:**
- Rogers says production could fill a whole other book, but you should still think about **how** you'll build the game.

### The vertical slice
- A **vertical slice** = a **level or sequence** of your game that's been **designed, built, and polished to the highest playability possible**.
- Every discipline is at the **best quality the final game will reach**: **controls, camera, visuals, gameplay, code, effects and audio**.
- **Target quality: usually ~80% of the final game.**
- **Two jobs:** it's a **demo for publishers** *and* a **template for the rest of the game** ("make everything else look like this").
- **Cost:** experienced teams can make one in a **few months**, but it's **very time-consuming** and can lead to **crunch time** — long, stressful hours rushing to create, insert, and test content.
- 🖼️ Cartoon (p. 423): a wide-eyed woman holding out a pie, asking for another vertical *slice*. (Yes, it's a pie pun. Use it: one slice shows every layer of the pie.)

### The horizontal layer
- A **horizontal layer** = the team gets **every element of the game, start to finish, running together** ("up") as a rough **gray box** build.
- **Gray box** levels: geometry **roughly built**, character animation mostly **placeholders**; **texturing, effects, and sound are generally absent**.
- Once all assets have been **playtested and approved**, the team moves on to **"prettying up"** and polishing further.
- 🖼️ Cartoon (p. 423): two stick figures in an empty box room with a few blocky steps; the caption begs you to imagine stone walls, skull torches, a spike-filled pit and goblin henchmen.

### Gray box: the problem and the payoff
- **Problem:** presenting gray box to others (teammates, publishers, marketing partners...) **requires imagination** — and Rogers says that ability is **rarer than you'd think**, even in a creative industry.
  - **Fix:** don't let people get the **wrong idea** about your game. Keep **concept art on hand** to communicate the intended visuals before they're in the game.
- **Payoff:** it gives the team a chance to **play around and discover what's great** about the game.
  - Book example: if **bouncing off enemies' heads turns out to be more fun than fighting them**, it's **not too late** to adjust the design around that.

### Side by side
| | Vertical slice | Horizontal layer (gray box) |
|---|---|---|
| What's built | One level/sequence, nearly final | Whole game, start to end, rough |
| Quality | ~80% of final, all disciplines | Placeholder art/anim, no textures, FX or sound |
| Best for | Selling to publishers; setting the quality bar/template | Finding the fun; testing flow and pacing early |
| Main risk | Time-consuming → crunch | Viewers lack imagination → wrong impression |
| Mitigation | Plan it into the schedule | Concept art on hand |

**🎮 Book examples:** The "bouncing on enemy heads" discovery → gray-box iteration lets you pivot cheaply toward what's actually fun.

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- Typical F2P production stages (studios differ, but the shape is common):
  1. **Concept** + market research (and sometimes a marketability test of the idea or art style).
  2. **Prototype** — gray box the **core gameplay** and **core loop**. Is the 30-second loop fun on a phone, one-handed, in short sessions?
  3. **Vertical slice / alpha** — the first ~10–30 minutes (FTUE + early levels + first meta step) at near-final quality, because that's what players and soft-launch metrics judge first.
  4. **Soft launch** — release in a few test countries; measure retention, monetization, CPI; iterate.
  5. **Global launch** — scale UA if KPIs clear the bar.
  6. **LiveOps** — events, seasons, new content — for years (or until sunset).
- Mobile twist: F2P often does **both** — horizontal gray box to prove the *loop* and *economy*, then a vertical slice of the **FTUE**, since first impressions drive D1 retention.
- "Imagination is rare" in mobile = **ad creatives** and **store screenshots**. Players won't imagine anything — your first second of video must look final.

**🪝 Make it stick:** **Vertical = one slice of the finished pie (all layers, tiny piece). Horizontal = the whole pie tin, filled with plain dough.** One tells you how it'll *taste*; the other tells you if it'll *fit the table*. 🥧

> ⚠️ **2010 → 2026:** The vocabulary is still standard. What's changed for mobile: the "publisher demo" moment is often replaced by **metrics gates** (prototype test → soft launch), and many F2P studios work in short iterative cycles where gray box is constantly tested with real players.

## 9. "Emergent, eschmergent!" — no such thing as accidental design
**🧠 Big idea:** **Emergent gameplay** is gameplay that "just happens" when players get a toolbox and freedom. Fine — but designers often use it as an **excuse to underdesign** and hope players "find the fun." Rogers: good design is **planned**; you should be able to predict how your systems interact.

**📋 The details:**
- **Emergent gameplay (definition):** play that "just happens" once you hand the player a **toolbox of gameplay tools** and room to **experiment with them**.
- **The problem:** designers using emergence as an **excuse to underdesign**, hoping the player will "find the fun."
- **Rogers' stance — "emergent, eschmergent!":**
  - **Accidental design doesn't exist.**
  - Understanding **how every gameplay element interacts with the others** is *part of the job* of designing.
  - If you plan and think through how elements relate, **the outcomes can be predicted**.
  - Yes, odd interactions can come from **bugs and inconsistencies** — but **never plan your design around those**.
  - If something unexpected happens and **doesn't break the game**, enjoy the old industry joke: *it's not a bug, it's a feature!*
- 🖼️ Cartoon (p. 424): a boss asks a player-designer if it's fun yet; he answers that the fun is "emergent." Next panel: the boss asks if that means he has no idea — and he admits he has no idea. (That's the whole lesson in two panels.)
- **Closing line of the production section:** **planning ahead is the secret to successful game production.**

**🎮 Book examples:** None named beyond the cartoon; the head-bounce example in §8 shows the *healthy* version — you discover something in play, then you **deliberately design around it**.

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- F2P **economies** are where "let it emerge" becomes a catastrophe: unplanned resource interactions create **exploits**, **inflation**, or **pay-to-win spirals**. Model sources and sinks in a **spreadsheet or simulation** before launch.
- Systemic games still work on mobile (physics puzzlers, sandbox experiences on Roblox) — but their designers planned the *rules* and tested the *interactions*.
- "It's not a bug, it's a feature" in F2P: a harmless quirk players love (a funny glitch, a speedrun trick) can become an **intentional mechanic or event**. A quirk that duplicates hard currency is just a bug. Hotfix it.

**🪝 Make it stick:** "The fun is emergent" is designer-speak for "I left the oven on and hope a cake appears." 🎂🔥 Emergence is the *dessert*, not the *recipe*.

## 10. What to Do for an Encore? — sequels as do-overs
**🧠 Big idea:** If your game reviews or sells well, you may get a **sequel**. Publishers love them because they're **safe(r) bets** — but too many teams waste them as lazy retreads. A sequel is your **chance to get it right.**

**📋 The details:**
- **When you get one:** if the game **reviews well**, **sells well**, or the publisher can **afford to pay you a second time**.
- **Publishers like sequels.** Rogers has worked on both the developer and publisher side, so he sees both angles.
- **Why publishers love them:** like Hollywood sequels, they're **safe(r) bets** — **proven intellectual property (IP)** that doesn't need explaining to the audience. The pitch writes itself: loved the first one? You'll love this one.
- **The problem:** sequels often turn into a **missed opportunity**:
  - Some teams **"phone it in"** or **rush** the sequel.
  - Result: **retreads** of the previous game.
- **Why a sequel should be *better*:** the first time you make a game you're limited by many things —
  - building a **team**,
  - building an **engine**,
  - figuring out **what the character and gameplay even are**,
  - getting the whole thing to **work** and actually be **fun**,
  - then **hoping the audience likes and buys it**.
  - Once that's done, you've **beaten the hard part** — especially if it sold well enough to earn a sequel.
- **Mindset:** start sequel **preproduction** with a driving desire to make the game **perfect**.
  - 📝 Footnote: you'll probably never reach perfection, but it's a fine target... though aiming for perfection famously didn't save ***Duke Nukem Forever*** (a byword for a game stuck in development for ages).
- **Rogers' Maximo story:** after the first ***Maximo***, he gave his producer a list of **40 things** he thought were broken and needed fixing in the sequel. He got **39 of the 40** fixed. The **first game sold better**, but he still believes the **second is the better game** — and that better game only existed because there was a sequel.
- **Great games that are sequels:** no sequels, no ***Grand Theft Auto 3***, no ***Call of Duty: Modern Warfare***, no ***Burnout Paradise***, no ***Curse of Monkey Island***, no ***Resident Evil 2*** or ***4***, no ***Lego Star Wars*** original-trilogy game.
- 🖼️ The section opens with title art for **"Dude With Sword 2"** (the book's sample game, now with a volcano). *Observation, not Rogers' claim:* it's a fun bit of irony, since one of his rules below is "don't just call it GameName 2."

**🎮 Book examples:**
- Hollywood sequels → proven IP is low-risk for financiers.
- *Maximo* → *Maximo vs. Army of Zin*: a 40-item fix list, 39 fixed; better game, lower sales.
- GTA3, CoD: Modern Warfare, Burnout Paradise, Curse of Monkey Island, RE2, RE4, Lego Star Wars Original Trilogy → many all-time greats are sequels.
- *Duke Nukem Forever* → perfectionism without shipping is its own trap.

**📱 F2P mobile translation — sequels vs. LiveOps:**
- In F2P, the default "encore" is **not a sequel — it's LiveOps.** The game is a **service**: new levels, events, seasons, heroes, and features keep the *same* app alive for years. *Candy Crush Saga* and *Clash of Clans* have been updated for over a decade instead of being replaced by numbered sequels.
- **Why F2P avoids sequels:** a new app has to rebuild its **install base** from zero (paying UA again), it can **cannibalize** the original's players, and players don't want to abandon years of **progression and purchases**.
- **When F2P does do "sequels,"** they're usually **spin-offs or successor games** that reuse a proven formula with a new twist and a new name: *Clash Royale* (same Clash universe, new real-time card-battle genre), *Homescapes* (the *Gardenscapes* match-3 + renovation formula moved indoors), *Royal Kingdom* (Dream Games' follow-up to *Royal Match*), *Candy Crush Soda Saga* (a variant of the *Candy Crush* formula). A numbered sequel like *Angry Birds 2* also exists — it's just less common.
- **"Sequel as do-over" in LiveOps:** your 40-item fix list doesn't wait for a sequel — it becomes the **roadmap** for updates, a **"2.0" relaunch**, or a **meta overhaul**.

**🪝 Make it stick:** A sequel is the game's **New Game+**: you start with your team, engine and gameplay already unlocked — so there's no excuse to play it on easy mode. 🔁

## 11. The sequel rulebook — spine, 30/70, don't disappoint
**🧠 Big idea:** Rogers' recipe for a good sequel: **keep the spine, fix the broken bits (30% new / 70% proven), give fans what they loved, give it a real name, add at least five new things — but don't drift so far that fans feel betrayed. Late in a franchise, consider a bold reinvention.**

**📋 The details:**
1. **Use the "spine" of the original** as the basis for the sequel's gameplay design.
   - **Keep everything good and improve it. Throw away everything bad.**
   - Sounds like common sense, but it isn't common: teams **"justify"** a lousy **camera**, bad **controls**, or weak **mechanics** simply because they were in the first game.
   - Being in the first game **doesn't make it good**. Don't be afraid to cut the bad bits — if the result is better than the original, **nobody will complain**.
   - **The 30/70 rule:** Rogers' friend, project manager **George Collins**, recommends every sequel be **30% new material and ideas, 70% based on the original game**. Rogers calls it a solid formula.
2. **Don't let the player down.**
   - Fans **expect certain things** in the sequel — don't disappoint them.
   - Example: if your audience loved the **wall-running** mechanic in the first game, **keep wall running** in the sequel.
3. **Name it something other than "GameName 2."**
   - **Names are really important** to a game.
   - Rogers praises the **Batman** films (e.g., *Batman Returns*, *Batman Begins*) and the **"Indiana Jones and the..."** films: their titles stay **mysterious** and **extend the fiction** instead of reducing it to a numbered outing.
4. **Always introduce something new...**
   - It may feel like pandering to marketing, but make sure there are **five new things** for the **back of the box** — preferably **new gameplay concepts** that bring something fresh.
   - Try to introduce **at least one new hero and one new villain** to the franchise.
   - **Remind the player this is a new experience**, not a rehash.
5. **...but go easy on the novelty.**
   - In ***Maximo vs. Army of Zin***, the team didn't realize players wanted **more supernatural enemies**; they made them fight **clockwork creatures** instead. **Fans were unhappy** because the sequel strayed from what they liked about the first game.
6. **The further down the road, the more room for change.**
   - Deep into a franchise, why not try something **wildly different**?
   - Requires **buy-in from your publisher and marketing**, so it's tricky.
   - Rogers has worked on franchises in their **5th, 8th, even 16th** incarnation; sometimes a **completely new direction** is exactly what's needed to shake things up.
   - It worked for the ***Grand Theft Auto*** and ***Wolfenstein*** franchises.
   - 📝 Footnote: it **may not work** either — **nothing is ever guaranteed** in video games.

**🎮 Book examples:**
- Lousy camera/controls carried over "because it was in the first one" → legacy is not a justification.
- Wall-running → keep the thing fans loved.
- Batman / Indiana Jones film titles → names that further the fiction.
- *Maximo vs. Army of Zin* clockwork enemies → too much novelty in the wrong place.
- GTA, Wolfenstein → late-franchise reinvention can pay off.

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
| Book sequel rule | F2P LiveOps / successor version |
|---|---|
| Keep the spine, cut the bad | Big updates keep the **core loop** sacred; fix pain points (bad UX, grindy walls, confusing currencies) players complain about in reviews and support tickets. |
| 30/70 | A healthy **season or update** is mostly familiar systems with a fresh layer: new event mechanic, new heroes, new map — not a new game. Successor games (like *Homescapes* after *Gardenscapes*) follow the same idea at app level. |
| Don't let the player down | **Never nerf or remove what players paid for or loved** without care and compensation. Breaking that trust shows up immediately in churn and review scores. |
| Name it right | In F2P naming = **ASO**. Season and event names should sell the fiction ("Pirate Season," not "Season 7"). Successor apps get distinct names (*Clash Royale*, *Royal Kingdom*) rather than "2." |
| Five new things | Every season pitch needs a **"what's new" list** for the update notes, store page and ad creatives — ideally new *gameplay*, plus a new character or boss. |
| Not too new | Changing the core genre or art style mid-life alienates your existing spenders. Test big changes with **A/B tests** or a separate mode first. |
| Late reinvention | Old F2P games sometimes get **major meta overhauls or relaunches** — high risk, needs leadership buy-in, can revive or kill a game. |

**🪝 Make it stick — the sequel has a SPINE:**
- **S**pine kept (good stays, bad goes — **30/70**)
- **P**layer expectations honored (keep the wall-running)
- **I**dentity in the name (not "GameName 2")
- **N**ew stuff: 5 back-of-box features + a new hero & villain
- **E**asy on the novelty (remember the clockwork enemies)
- ...and if the franchise is old enough: dare to go wildly different.

## 🏆 Universal Truths & Clever Ideas
All 14 of Level 17's end-of-chapter truths, in my words:

1. **Demo > GDD > pitch presentation > pitch outline.** Playable beats written beats slides beats talk.

11. **Don't count on emergent gameplay to make your game fun. Good design is planned in advance.**
    - 📱 so for F2P: model your economy; never "let it emerge."
12. **Follow the 30/70 rule for sequels:** keep what was great in the first game, drop what didn't work.
13. **Don't let legacy hold your sequel back.** Honestly assess what can be improved from the original.
14. **Don't let the player down.**
    - 📱 so for F2P: protect what players invested in — progress, purchases, favorite modes.

## 13. Achievement Unlocked: Exactly Like Making Chili
**🧠 Big idea:** The book's very last pages are... an actual chili recipe — no commentary, just food. It's the payoff to Rogers' running joke from Level 4: **making games is exactly like making chili.** Here's the recipe in my words, plus the analogy it pays off.

**📋 The details — the recipe (paraphrased; serves about 6–8, the book's "Feeds 6 to 8"):**
- **Ingredient groups (with the book's amounts):**
  - **Base:** about ¼ cup of extra virgin olive oil and 2 fresh garlic cloves.
  - **Meat:** about 3 pounds of beef brisket, all fat trimmed.
  - **Liquid:** two large cans of crushed tomatoes and two small cans of beef broth.
  - **Heat & spice:** two ancho chilis, plus ½ tbsp chili powder, 1 tbsp cayenne pepper, ½ tbsp crushed red chili (seeds included) and 1 tbsp garlic salt (the book names the Lowry's brand). Cayenne and garlic salt get the biggest spoonfuls.
  - **Veg:** two large green peppers, one large red pepper, one large yellow onion.
  - **Toppings (for serving):** ¼ cup scallions, ½ cup sour cream, ½ cup shredded Monterey Jack cheese.
  - Count check: **16 ingredient lines**, a **9-step** method, and the only timings are **7–10 min browning** and **2–2½ hours simmering**.
- **The 9 steps (own words):**
  1. Slice the garlic, warm the oil in a big pot or Dutch oven over medium heat, brown the garlic, then add the brisket and brown it (roughly 7–10 minutes).
  2. While that's going, blend the ancho chilis with the beef broth into a purée; set it aside.
  3. Carefully move the browned meat to a plate, pour off the oil, then add the chili purée and the crushed tomatoes (juice included).
  4. Stir in the dry seasonings: garlic salt, chili powder, cayenne, crushed red chili.
  5. Bring it to a boil, then turn it down and simmer covered for about 2 to 2½ hours, until the meat is fully cooked and tender.
  6. Cut the peppers (stems and seeds out) and the onion into big chunks.
  7. Pull the meat out, shred it with two forks, return it to the pot with the peppers and onion, and adjust seasoning to taste.
  8. Turn the heat up and boil off most of the liquid.
  9. Ladle into bowls, top with cheese, sour cream and scallions — serve and enjoy.

**🔗 The analogy this pays off (📖 = Rogers' own mapping from Level 4, pp. 57–59 · 🧪 = my extension to the recipe steps):**
| Chili | Game development |
|---|---|
| 📖 The **recipe** | Your **documentation** (one-sheet, ten-pager, beat chart, GDD): it says what goes in *and how to make it*. Follow it — but expect it to change if something goes wrong. You're making chili, not soup or stew. |
| 📖 **Season to taste** | Some parts of the game are "meatier" — adjust the design to make them more pronounced. |
| 📖 **Ingredients + equipment** (pots, spoons, stove) | **People and tools.** Sometimes you have what you want; sometimes you improvise — cowboys made good chili with a campfire and a tin can. |
| 📖 **Combining ingredients** | Chili forgives chopping style (careful vs. thrown in), just like productions can be methodical or a mad dash — but follow the recipe so nothing's forgotten, and do things **in the right order** (always **brown the meat first** — Rogers learned that the hard way). |
| 📖 **The boil** | **Crunch time:** a frantic burst to get everything in and running. Boil too long and you ruin the chili, burn the pot, set the stove on fire — games and studios have been destroyed by too much crunch. |
| 📖 **The simmer** | **Polish and bug fixing:** chili isn't ready the moment it's assembled. Iterate, improve, season; allow time for it. Let the team play to see what works. |
| 📖 **Tastes better the next day** | Time and iteration improve the result. |
| 📖 **Last-minute rescue** | Troubled games can sometimes be saved at the eleventh hour — but don't plan on it (stomach upset!). |
| 📖 **Ugly but delicious** | A game that isn't pretty can still entertain if the **gameplay** is good. Good games and good chili satisfy the soul. |
| 📖 (Level 12) **Beans** | **Hazards** are the beans: filler when there isn't enough meat, and they help make the level's "music." |
| 🧪 Step 2: purée in parallel | **Parallel pipelines** — art and audio prep while code builds the base. |
| 🧪 Step 3: pour off the oil | **Cut the fat** — trim content that isn't pulling its weight (§7). |
| 🧪 Step 5: 2+ hours covered simmer | **Soft launch** — slow, patient iteration with the lid on (limited markets) before you serve the world. |
| 🧪 Step 7: shred + season to taste | **Tuning and balancing** based on what players actually taste (data). |
| 🧪 Step 8: boil off the liquid | **Final tightening** — remove filler so what's left is concentrated. |
| 🧪 Step 9: toppings | **Presentation** — icon, store page, juice and UI polish: the first thing players see. |
| 🧪 Feeds 6–8 | Know your **audience size** — a recipe (and a game) is designed for specific people. |

**🎮 Book examples:** The Level 4 story: a visiting game director claimed making games was like *fishing*; Rogers decided it was nothing like fishing and invented the chili analogy instead. This chapter is the book literally handing you the recipe as a final "achievement."

**📱 F2P mobile translation:** F2P chili never leaves the stove. After you serve it (global launch), you keep **adding ingredients** (LiveOps content), **re-seasoning** (balance patches, A/B tests), and **managing the heat** (event cadence without permanent crunch). The danger is the *boil that never ends* — design a cadence your kitchen can sustain.

**🪝 Make it stick:** **Recipe → Ingredients → Brown first → Boil (briefly!) → Simmer (long) → Season → Serve.** If you remember nothing else from the whole book, remember: *don't leave the pot on full boil.* 🌶️🔥

## 14. 🧪 F2P bonus: pitch deck, soft launch & kill gates
*(Not in the book — this is the 2026 F2P translation of Level 17's pitch and production advice. Bonus Level 9 has the book's full slide outline.)*

**🧠 Big idea:** In F2P, the pitch doesn't end when someone says yes. Every stage — concept, prototype, soft launch, global launch — is a **new pitch judged by numbers**, and a good team decides *in advance* which numbers mean "scale it" and which mean "kill it."

### The F2P pitch deck (Rogers' 5 basics, upgraded)
| # | Slide | What goes in it | Book ancestor |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Title + hook** | Logo/key art, one-line high concept ("a cozy merge game where you restore a haunted lighthouse") | Title screen with logo |
| 2 | **Team** | Who you are, relevant shipped games, why *you* can make this | Company profile |
| 3 | **High concept & audience** | Genre, "X meets Y" comparables, target player and their motivation, platform, session length | High concept |
| 4 | **Market fit** | Genre size and trend, top competitors and how they perform (from market-intelligence tools), the gap you fill, your differentiator | Proof it'll make money |
| 5 | **Core gameplay + core loop** | The 30-second action and the session loop, as a diagram + a GIF | A few pages of details |
| 6 | **Meta & progression** | What players build or collect over weeks and months | Details |
| 7 | **Retention hypothesis** | Why they return on D1, D7, D30 (streaks, events, social, collection goals) | Proof it'll be awesome |
| 8 | **Monetization hypothesis** | What players buy and *why* (IAP, ads, pass), main sinks, spender-tier thinking, ethical guardrails (odds disclosure, no predatory pressure) | Proof it'll make money |
| 9 | **LiveOps plan** | Event types, season cadence, content pipeline (the modern version of BL9's "download strategy" slide) | Download strategy (BL9) |
| 10 | **Marketability / UA** | The ad hook, planned creative tests, CPI risk of the art style | — |
| 11 | **KPI targets & kill criteria** | Target D1/D7/D30, conversion, ARPDAU; what numbers at which milestone mean stop | — |
| 12 | **Production specs** | Team size, timeline to prototype/soft launch, budget | Production specs (BL9) |
| 13 | **Proof** | Prototype video or build, playtest results, CPI test results | Game demo (top of the pitch equation) |

- 📖 **Book's Slide 9 — Production Specs (the last slide of the BL9 outline):** share your production plans by answering three questions: **how big is the team dedicated to the game, how long will it take to make, and how much will it cost?** (Its predecessor, Slide 8, says most games now need **download support** after release to extend their life — the seed of the LiveOps slide above.) In F2P, add the milestone dates: prototype, soft launch, global launch.

### The F2P greenlight funnel (ASCII)
```
IDEA ──► CONCEPT DECK ──► PROTOTYPE ──► SOFT LAUNCH ──► GLOBAL LAUNCH ──► LIVEOPS
           │ market fit      │ is it fun?    │ do they stay?    │ does UA scale?   │ does it keep growing?
           ▼                 ▼               ▼ do they pay?     ▼ LTV > CPI?       ▼
          KILL?             KILL?           KILL / ITERATE    KILL / HOLD        SUNSET (someday)
```
- **Concept gate:** is there a market? Does the hook test well (art/creative tests)?
- **Prototype gate:** is the core loop fun in gray box? Do playtesters ask to keep playing?
- **Soft launch gate:** release in a few test countries; measure **retention** (D1/D7/D30), **monetization** (conversion, ARPDAU), **CPI**, and **LTV projections**. Iterate for weeks or months.
  - Commonly quoted *rough* ballparks for casual games are around 35–40% D1, roughly 15% D7 and 5–8% D30; midcore bars differ, and every publisher has its own. Treat these as conversation starters, not law.
- **Global launch gate:** can you buy players profitably (**LTV > CPI**) at scale?
- **Kill decisions:** the healthiest studios kill early and celebrate the learning. Supercell is famous for this — it has publicly ended several games in soft launch or beta (e.g., *Rush Wars*, *Clash Quest*, *Clash Mini*) instead of shipping something it didn't believe would last for years.
- **Rogers link:** "If something doesn't work, throw it out — but plan ahead" (truth #9) is exactly a kill gate. **Write the kill criteria in the pitch**, *before* you fall in love with the game (that's design blinders insurance).

### Crunch and the LiveOps cadence
- In boxed games, crunch was a phase before ship. In LiveOps, **the deadline comes back every week** — a new event, a new offer, a new season.
- Anti-crunch tools (Rogers' "responsible design goals + detailed planning," updated):
  - **Event templates** — reusable formats re-skinned with new themes.
  - **Content buffer** — several weeks of finished events banked ahead.
  - **Calendar planning** months ahead, with slack for sickness and holidays ("made by people").
  - **Scope the cadence to the team**, not the team to the cadence.

**🪝 Make it stick:** A kill gate is a **fire exit you install before the fire.** Nobody plans the exit *during* the blaze. 🚪🔥

## 15. 🧪 F2P bonus: breaking in as a junior F2P designer
*(Not in the book — it applies Rogers' "pitch equation" and his closing career advice to getting your first F2P design job.)*

**🧠 Big idea:** Hiring is a pitch where **you** are the game. The pitch equation still rules: **playable proof > written design > slides > talk.**

**📋 Your career "pitch equation":**
1. **Playable stuff (the "demo"):** small prototypes — a core-loop toy, a puzzle mechanic, a jam game. Finished and tiny beats ambitious and broken.
2. **Written design (the "GDD"):** 2–4 tight design docs — a feature spec (e.g., a new limited-time event for a real game), an **economy spreadsheet** (sources, sinks, progression curve), a FTUE redesign.
3. **Teardowns (your "presentation"):** structured breakdowns of live F2P games. A strong teardown covers:
   - **Core gameplay & core loop** (diagram it)
   - **Meta & progression** (what's the long-term goal?)
   - **FTUE** (first 10 minutes: what's taught, when, how)
   - **Economy** (currencies, sources, sinks, energy/timers)
   - **Monetization** (offers, pass, ads, price points, starter pack timing)
   - **Retention & social** (daily rewards, streaks, events, guilds)
   - **LiveOps calendar** (what changed this week?)
   - **"What I'd change"** — with a hypothesis and the KPI it should move
4. **Talk (the "pitch"):** your interviews and design tests.

**🧪 Design tests (common take-home formats):**
- "Design a new event / feature for *our* game" → show you understand their loop and players.
- "Our D7 retention dropped — diagnose and propose fixes" → funnel thinking and hypotheses.
- "Balance this economy / tune this progression" → spreadsheet skills.
- Apply Rogers' pitch rules: **right audience** (their game, their players), **know it inside out** (play it for days first), **keep the focus** (one strong idea, fully developed beats five sketches), **cut the twiddley bits** (a crisp 5–8 pages beats 40).

**🤝 Rogers' closing rules, applied to your career:**
- **Be fair and generous:** the F2P world is small. Share teardowns publicly, credit others, help people — it compounds.
- **Keep playing games, even bad ones:** play top-grossing games *and* games that failed soft launch. Write down why each succeeded or failed.
- **Take breaks:** a burnt-out junior makes worse design tests.
- **Enjoy it:** curiosity shows in interviews.

**🪝 Make it stick:** Your portfolio is a **vertical slice of you**: a small piece, polished to ~80% of the designer you're becoming. 🍰
