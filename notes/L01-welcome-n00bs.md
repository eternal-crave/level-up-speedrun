# 🐣 Level 1 — Welcome, N00bs!
> 📖 Book pp. 1–22 · ⏱️ ~60 min study · 📱 F2P relevance ★★★★☆ — what a game is, the one-sentence objective test, where mobile F2P came from, genre literacy for pitching and teardowns, and who does what on a team (with the modern F2P version of every job).

## ⚡ TL;DR — the 30-second version
- **A game = an activity with at least one player, rules, and a victory condition.** A video game is that, played on a screen. Academic definitions overcomplicate it.
- **If you can't state the objective in one sentence, like the back of a board-game box, you have a problem.** (Danny Bilson's rule.)
- **History is one long technology chase:** labs → arcades → consoles, handhelds and PCs → (after the book) phones and F2P. Each platform's hardware created new genres. Adapt or get left behind.
- **Genres describe the style of gameplay**, and they keep splitting and blending. Mixing genres is how new ones are born, and mobile F2P is a festival of hybrids.
- **Games are made by specialists:** programmers, artists, designers, producers, testers, composers, sound designers, writers, plus a whole publishing side. In F2P, add systems/economy, level, LiveOps and monetization designers, product managers, data analysts and UA.
- **The designer's job:** create the ideas and rules, explain *why* something is good or bad, and own whether the game is fun.

## 1. What is a game?
**🧠 Big idea:** Forget fancy academic definitions. A pastime becomes a game the moment you add **rules** and a **way to win** (which automatically creates a way to fail). A video game is simply a game on a screen.

**📋 The details:**
- **Who this chapter is for:** people new to games and how they're made. You can skip it if you're not a n00b, but Rogers warns you'll miss good stuff.
  - Footnote: **n00b** is short for "newbie," someone new to a game. The word is older than the Internet but caught on in online multiplayer communities. It isn't flattering, since it implies inexperience. Rogers jokes that only a real n00b would read a footnote explaining it.
- **Academic definitions Rogers thinks try too hard:**
  - **Chris Crawford** (*The Art of Computer Game Design*, 1982): a game is a closed, formal system that subjectively represents a slice of reality.
  - **Kevin Langdon** (*The New Thesaurus*, 1979): games need players in conflict with each other.
  - **Bernard Suits** (*The Grasshopper: Games, Life and Utopia*, 1978): playing a game is "a voluntary effort to overcome unnecessary obstacles." Rogers finds it amusing but still too scholarly.
- **The hand ball test** (a person bouncing a ball off a wall):
  - It needs only **one player**, so who's in conflict? That breaks Langdon's definition.
  - Bouncing a ball isn't much of a **metaphor for reality** (unless your life is very boring). That breaks Crawford's. Sometimes a ball bouncing off a wall is just a ball bouncing off a wall.
  - On its own it's a **time-waster**. It becomes a **game** when you add **rules and an objective**:
    - **Rule** examples: throw with your right hand and catch with your left, or never drop the ball.
    - **Victory condition** example: catch the ball **10 times in a row**.
    - **Failure state:** breaking any rule or failing the victory condition.
  - Once those criteria exist, you've created a game.
  - Fun fact: something this simple inspired one of the earliest video games, **Tennis for Two**. *(Visual: the book draws it as a ball bouncing over a little net on a round oscilloscope screen in a boxy panel.)*
- **Rogers' definitions:**
  - **Q: What is a game?** **A:** an activity that (1) **requires at least one player**, (2) **has rules**, and (3) **has a victory condition**. That's it.
  - **Q: What is a video game?** **A:** a game played on a **video screen**.
  - You *can* complicate it with devices, peripherals, control schemes, player metrics, boss fights and zombies, and later Levels do. But the core is that simple.

**🎮 Book examples:**
- **Hand ball**: one player, no conflict, no reality simulation, yet it's a real game once rules and a goal exist.
- **Tennis for Two**: a hand-ball-simple idea on an oscilloscope, one of the first video games.

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- Use the three-part test on any F2P mode:
  - **Candy Crush–style level:** player ✅, rules (swap adjacent candies, limited moves) ✅, victory condition (hit the level goal before moves run out) ✅. The **failure state** (out of moves) is also where monetization lives, because extra moves are offered there.
  - **Idle games** stretch the definition. They have rules and progress but often no *final* victory condition, so designers add **short-term victory conditions** (stage clears, chapter goals, event milestones) to keep a game-like structure.
- **Failure states matter in F2P:** in many F2P games the failure screen is where the player decides whether to retry, wait, watch an ad or pay. Design it deliberately and ethically. It *is* gameplay (→ L00).
- **Live-game twist:** F2P games run for years with no final ending, so they stack **nested victory conditions**: per level, per event, per season, per collection.

**🪝 Make it stick:** **P-R-V = "Play Requires Victory":** **P**layer, **R**ules, **V**ictory condition. Picture a lone person bouncing a ball off a wall, counting "8… 9… 10!" and spiking it like a touchdown. That's a game.

> ⚠️ **2010 → 2026:** "Played on a video screen" covers phones, tablets, VR headsets and even smartwatches. The definition holds. What changed is *where* and *for how long* people play: in a few minutes stolen from a commute, between other things.

## 2. The objective test (Danny Bilson's box rule)
**🧠 Big idea:** You should be able to state a game's **objective** quickly and clearly. If you can't, the design has a problem.

**📋 The details:**
- Danny Bilson (THQ's EVP of Core Games, who also wrote the foreword) has a rule of thumb: sum up your game's objective as simply as the old **Milton Bradley board games** did **on the front of the box**.
- Examples (paraphrased from real box copy):
  - **Battleship:** sink every one of your opponent's ships. ✅ Crystal clear.
  - **Operation:** successful operations earn money, and failures set off an alarm. ✅ Clear.
  - **Mouse Trap:** the box describes a Rube Goldberg chain of a crank, gears, a lever, a stop sign, a shoe, a bucket, a metal ball, rickety stairs, a rain pipe, a "helping hand" rod, a bowling ball, a "thing-a-ma-jig," a bathtub, a diving board, a diver flipping into a wash tub, and finally a cage dropping onto the mouse. ❌ The joke counterexample.
- **Lesson:** keep game objectives **simple**.

**🎮 Book examples:** Battleship and Operation (good: one breath). Mouse Trap (bad: a paragraph of machinery).

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- Your objective has to survive **three tiny boxes**: the **store page subtitle**, the first **3 seconds of an ad**, and the **first tutorial screen**.
  - "Match 3 to clear the board and restore the mansion" (the Homescapes/Gardenscapes formula).
  - "Destroy the enemy towers before they destroy yours" (Clash Royale).
  - "Be the last one standing" (battle royale).
- **The F2P Mouse Trap trap:** midcore games with 12 currencies, 8 upgrade paths and 5 event types at once. Each extra system needs its own one-line objective, or players drown. Introduce systems gradually during onboarding (**FTUE**) so the *current* objective is always obvious.
- **Ad creatives and playable ads live or die on this.** If a viewer can't grasp the goal in about 3 seconds, they scroll past.

**🪝 Make it stick:** **Battleship, not Mouse Trap.** If explaining your objective requires a bathtub and a diving board, start over.

## 3. A brief history of video games
**🧠 Big idea:** Video games went from giant lab computers to arcades to living rooms and pockets. Every jump in hardware (display type, controller, storage, input device) **created new genres and new ways to make money**. That's why one of the chapter's Universal Truths is "adapt or get left behind."

### 3a. The lab era (1950s–1970s)
**📋 The details:**
- The 1950s brought TV, 3-D movies and rock 'n' roll, and video games too. Very few people played them, on **very large computers**.
- **The first game programmers:** students in university computer labs (like **MIT**) and employees of military research sites like **Brookhaven National Laboratory**.
- **Early games:** **OXO** (1952), **Spacewar!** (1962), **Colossal Cave** (1976). They had very simple graphics, or none at all (text only).
- They were displayed on very small **black-and-white oscilloscope screens**.
- *(Visual: three people crowded around a room-sized computer with a round oscilloscope screen. Gaming started as a lab hobby.)*

**📱 F2P link:** these were made by tinkerers for fun with no business model, a lot like jam games and prototypes today. **Colossal Cave**, a text adventure, is a distant ancestor of today's interactive-story mobile apps.

### 3b. The arcade boom (1971 → early 1980s)
**📋 The details:**
- Future Atari founders **Ted Dabney and Nolan Bushnell** played **Spacewar!** at the **University of Utah** computer lab and were inspired to make **Computer Space** (1971), the **first arcade video game**.
- Despite the name, early arcade games lived in **bars**. Dedicated video arcades appeared by the **late 1970s**.
- **Two kinds of graphics:**
  - **Vector graphics**: images built from **lines**. Examples: **Asteroids**, **Battlezone**, **Star Castle**. *(Visual: Asteroids' outlined rocks and triangle ship.)*
  - **Raster graphics**: images built from a **grid of dots called pixels**, in color. *(Visual: Galaxian's rows of colorful pixel aliens.)*
- Color raster graphics made **cartoon-style characters** possible. Two of them, **Pac-Man** (Namco, 1980) and **Donkey Kong** (Nintendo, 1982), turned into pop-culture icons almost overnight.
- **Three cabinet styles dominated early-1980s arcades:**
  - **Uprights**: you **stand** in front of the cabinet.
  - **Cocktail tables**: the game is set into the top of a small **table** and you **sit** to play.
  - **Cockpits**: elaborate cabinets you **lean into or sit inside** for a more immersive experience.
  - *(Visual: an upright "GAME!" cabinet, a sit-down cocktail table, and a spaceship-style cockpit with a seated pilot.)*

**📱 F2P link:** pixels made **characters**, and characters made **brands**. Pac-Man shows why mobile F2P invests so heavily in **mascots** (Candy Crush's candies, Royal Match's King Robert, Angry Birds' birds). A readable character is marketing that's always on screen.

### 3c. Arcade spectacle and decline (mid-1980s → late 1990s)
**📋 The details:**
- **Mid-1980s:** arcades sprang up everywhere and video games became a worldwide craze. **Genres and themes diversified.** Controls and cabinets became **more elaborate**, with realistic controllers and beautifully decorated, uniquely shaped cabinets:
  - **Tail Gunner** (Vectorbeam, 1979): two players sat **back to back** in a spaceship cockpit.
  - **Star Trek** (Sega, 1982): fight Klingons from a replica of **Captain Kirk's command chair**.
  - **Out Run** (Sega, 1986): drive an actual **Ferrari Testarossa** cabinet that **moved and shook**.
- **Late 1990s:** arcade games looked like **mini theme-park rides**: rideable race horses, **gyroscopic** motion simulators, fighting booths where you threw **real punches and kicks** at virtual enemies.
- **The peak: Virtual World's BattleTech Centers.** Steampunk-themed arcades with linked **"battle pods"** where **8 players** fought each other in giant virtual **mechs**.
  - Rogers' footnote (he visited several times in the mid-1990s): each pod was a **photo-booth-sized cockpit** with **dual joysticks and foot pedals** for movement, triggers and thumb switches for weapons, and banks of **dip switches** around the monitor that **all did something** (tracking devices, venting overheating weapons). It took about a **half-hour session just to learn the switches**, and he calls it the most realistic gaming experience he's had.
- **The decline:**
  - Elaborate machines needed **lots of floor space** and were **expensive to maintain**.
  - In the late 1990s, **home systems rivaled and then surpassed** arcade graphics.
  - Arcades **went out of business by the dozens**. Video games were replaced by more profitable **redemption machines** and skill games like **Skee-Ball**.
  - Footnote: **redemption machines** are claw-catcher "games" in US stores and supermarkets. Rogers would rather play the lottery, since they're rigged so you (almost) always lose. In **Japan**, though, he says they're **winnable** and stocked with cool prizes.
  - Many cabinets ended up with **private collectors**. The **golden age of arcades was over**.

**📱 F2P link:**
- **Arcades invented pay-per-attempt.** A quarter bought a life, and a "continue?" countdown asked for another. F2P's **"+5 moves?"** offer is the direct descendant: monetizing the moment of near-success.
- **Arcade high-score tables** became **leaderboards**, ranked ladders and weekly tournaments.
- **A warning from the claw machine:** a game that feels rigged destroys trust. Rogers' contrast between rigged US claws and fair Japanese ones is the same ethical line F2P walks with gacha odds and difficulty spikes tuned to sell boosters. Fair, transparent odds (and pity systems) keep players. Rigged-feeling ones burn them out, and loot-box rules in several countries now enforce some of this.
- **Spectacle vs cost:** BattleTech pods were amazing but too expensive to run. Mobile has its own version: building content costs more than it earns back. Live games survive by making content **cheap to produce and endlessly replayable**.

### 3d. Arcades reborn as social spaces
**📋 The details:**
- More recently, arcades turned into **social and virtual experiences**:
  - **LAN gaming centers**: retail plus social space where people pay **by the hour** to play PC and console games. Some upgraded to **large-scale events in movie-theater-sized venues**.
  - **Internet cafés**: like LAN centers but built around a **café-style** atmosphere.
- **Dark rides plus gameplay:** companies like **Disney** and the **Sally Corporation** merged classic amusement-park **dark rides** with games.
  - Footnote: a **dark ride** carries guests past scenes, usually with audio-animatronic characters, like Disneyland's **Pirates of the Caribbean** and **Haunted Mansion**.
  - **Toy Story Midway Mania** (Disney California Adventure, 2008): a **four-player cart** rolls past giant screens where riders compete in **carnival-style shooting games**, and riders get sprayed with **air and water** to match on-screen effects.
  - It came **full circle**: Toy Story Midway Mania was also released for the **Wii**.
- Rogers speculates that hybrid **arcade/amusement parks** full of virtual games and interactive dark rides might come next.

**📱 F2P link:**
- **Social play is the throughline.** LAN centers and internet cafés (huge in Asia) fed directly into **esports** and **mobile esports**. Games like Mobile Legends: Bang Bang and PUBG Mobile run major tournaments, especially in Southeast Asia.
- **Play in the physical world:** Pokémon GO did what Rogers imagined, turning real streets and parks into the play space, with community events that pull players outdoors together.

### 3e. Consoles
**📋 The details:**
- **Definition:** a **console** is a gaming platform for the **home**. A **microprocessor** runs it and sends a video signal to a **TV or monitor**.
  - Footnote exception: the **Vectrex** (Smith Engineering, 1982) had its processor, screen, controller and one built-in game all in **one self-contained portable unit**.
- **What made consoles different from arcades:**
  - **Controllers:** arcade machines had dedicated controls for one game. A console controller has **enough buttons, triggers and analog controls** for many kinds of games.
  - **Media:** early arcade motherboards held **one game**. Consoles use **cartridges, CDs and DVDs**, so you can switch games quickly.
- **Popular or notable earlier consoles (from the late 1970s on):** Atari 2600 and Jaguar, Mattel Intellivision, ColecoVision, Nintendo Entertainment System and Super Nintendo, Sega Genesis and Dreamcast, the 3DO.
- **Current consoles in 2010:** **PlayStation 3, Xbox 360, Nintendo Wii**, bringing games into millions of homes.
- *(Visual: sketches of the Atari 2600, NES and original PlayStation.)*

**📱 F2P link:** the console ideas that matter most for mobile are **"one device, many games"** (the app store took that to the extreme) and **swappable media**, which became **downloadable updates**. Live updates are what make LiveOps possible: the game in your pocket changes every week.

> ⚠️ **2010 → 2026:** PS3, Xbox 360 and Wii are three-plus generations old. Consoles went **digital-first** and adopted F2P themselves (Fortnite, Warzone, Genshin Impact on PlayStation). Many big F2P games are **cross-platform**, with mobile as just one of several screens.

### 3f. Handhelds and mobile
**📋 The details:**
- **Definition:** **handheld games** have a display, processor and controller like an arcade machine, but are **small enough to hold in your hands**.
- **First handhelds: one game per device.**
  - **Auto Race** (Mattel Electronics, 1976): a **digital** (LED-style) display.
  - **Game & Watch** series (Nintendo, 1980): a more appealing **LCD** screen.
- **Microvision** (Milton Bradley, 1979): one of the earliest handhelds with **swappable cartridges**.
- **Handhelds boomed** once **Tetris** caught fire on the **Game Boy** (Nintendo, 1989), the forerunner of the **Nintendo DS**.
  - Footnote: the DS resembles the original Game & Watch devices in several ways, and Rogers notes that's no coincidence. (My addition: think of the clamshell, dual-screen "Multi Screen" Game & Watch models.)
- **Recent handhelds are powerful:** the **Sony PSP** can run the equivalent of a **PlayStation One** game, a huge jump from the digital blips of **Mattel Football**.
- *(Visual: the Game Boy, Sega Nomad and PSP.)*
- **Mobile, in 2010:** handheld gaming, **especially on mobile devices**, is getting more popular. **Phone games are quick and cheaper to make**, and the number of mobile developers has **exploded** in a few years. Rogers asks: **are mobile games the future of gaming?**

**📱 F2P link: yes, they were.** Mobile grew into the **biggest segment of the games industry**, roughly half of global game revenue in recent years. And the phone combines everything above:
- **Game & Watch → the phone**: one device that now holds thousands of games.
- **Tetris on Game Boy → puzzle as the portable killer app**: the line runs straight to match-3 and **block puzzles** like Block Blast!.
- **"Quick and cheaper to make"** in 2010 became **hyper-casual** prototyping a few years later, where small teams build and test simple games in days or weeks.

### 3g. Personal computers (PCs)
**📋 The details:**
- PCs became popular in the **late 1970s**, and both **game programming and game playing** became more common.
- **Bedroom coders:** a whole generation of developers started out programming games in their bedrooms. Games were stored on **tape drives and floppy discs**.
- Early PC games tried to **copy arcade games**, but the **keyboard** allowed much richer input, which gave rise to new genres like the **text adventure**.
- **Longer play sessions** at a computer led to **longer experiences and simulations**: **construction and management** and **strategy** games became popular.
- As hardware, memory and storage evolved (**CD and DVD**), PC games became **more detailed, involved and complex**.
- The **mouse** made the **first-person shooter (FPS)** popular.
- By the **mid-1990s**, the PC was **the ultimate gaming platform**.
- Genres that stay strong on PC: **strategy**, **FPS**, and **massively multiplayer online games (MMOs)**.
- *(Visual: a Commodore 64, a Macintosh Plus and a modern tower PC.)*

**📱 F2P link:** the input device decides the genre. **Keyboard → text adventures. Mouse → FPS. Touchscreen → swipe, tap, drag, draw.** That's why mobile's native genres are **match-3, merge, runners, slicing games (Fruit Ninja), physics flicking (Angry Birds) and card dropping (Clash Royale)**. Genres that rely on precise aim had to invent **virtual sticks, auto-fire and aim assist** to come to mobile (Call of Duty: Mobile, PUBG Mobile).

### 3h. ➕ The missing chapter: how mobile F2P happened (2008–2026)
The book stops in 2010, right as the phone revolution began. Here's the lineage so you can see where *your* genre came from.

**🧬 Lineage table: old ancestor → what it contributed → mobile F2P descendant**

| Ancestor (book era) | What it contributed | Mobile F2P descendant |
|---|---|---|
| Arcades (quarter per life, "Continue?") | Pay per attempt, short sessions, high scores | Extra moves, revives, boosters, leaderboards, hyper-casual runs |
| Arcade characters (Pac-Man, Donkey Kong) | Readable mascots = brands | Mascot-led brands: Angry Birds, Candy Crush, Royal Match's king |
| Game & Watch / Game Boy + Tetris | Portable, pick-up-and-play puzzle | Match-3, block puzzles, tile-match |
| Tamagotchi / pet sims | Daily care, check-in habits | Daily logins, streaks, pet and idle games |
| PC construction/management | Build and expand with limited resources | Hay Day, Township, city builders, base building in Clash of Clans |
| PC strategy and RTS (the "4X") | Expand / explore / exploit / exterminate | Mobile 4X: Rise of Kingdoms, Whiteout Survival, Last War |
| MMORPGs (grinding, raids, PvP) | Persistent worlds, guilds, raids | Clans/alliances, co-op raid bosses, gacha RPG endgames |
| LAN centers / internet cafés | Social and competitive play | Mobile esports (MLBB, PUBG Mobile), squad play (Brawl Stars) |
| Text adventures | Choice-driven stories | Interactive-story and narrative-choice apps, event storylines |
| Party games (minigames) | Many players, quick competitive rounds | Stumble Guys, Among Us, minigame events |
| Dark rides / location play | Play in physical space | Pokémon GO and location-based events |

**🕰️ A short timeline (milestones; dates are well documented):**
- **1997: Snake** ships preinstalled on Nokia phones and, over the following years, ends up on a huge number of handsets. For many people it was their first game on a phone.
- **Late 2000s: Japanese feature-phone social platforms** (GREE, Mobage) popularize **gacha** monetization before smartphones take over.
- **2008:** Apple's **App Store** launches (July) and Android's market follows. Anyone can sell a game worldwide.
- **2009:** Facebook social games like **FarmVille** spread **free-to-play with energy, gifting and friend invites** to a mass audience. iOS starts allowing **in-app purchases in free apps** (late 2009). **Angry Birds** launches as a cheap paid app.
- **2010–2011:** premium mobile hits (Fruit Ninja, Cut the Rope, Temple Run). Prices race toward free, and **freemium** becomes the norm.
- **2012: the F2P big bang.** **Puzzle & Dragons** (gacha in Japan), **Candy Crush Saga** (Facebook, then mobile: lives, boosters, saga map), **Hay Day** and **Clash of Clans** (Supercell: timers, base building, clans). Japan cracks down on "kompu gacha" (complete-the-set gacha) the same year, an early sign of regulation.
- **2013–2014: Flappy Bird** shows how a tiny, brutally simple game can go viral.
- **2016: Clash Royale** (real-time PvP on mobile, chest timers) and **Pokémon GO** (location-based, AR).
- **2017–2019: the hyper-casual boom** (ad-monetized, one-mechanic games pushed by big publishers via test-and-scale UA: Helix Jump and many more). **Battle royale** reaches phones (PUBG Mobile, Free Fire, Fortnite). **Battle passes** spread.
- **2020: Genshin Impact** brings console-quality open-world gacha to phones. **Among Us** explodes.
- **2021:** Apple's **App Tracking Transparency** (iOS 14.5) shakes up ad targeting and UA, and pushes the industry toward **hybrid-casual** (casual gameplay plus deeper IAP and meta). **Royal Match** launches and takes on Candy Crush with polish and speed.
- **2022–2024:** Marvel Snap (fast card battler), **Monopoly GO!** (board-game "casino-lite" with huge social events), Squad Busters, **Pokémon TCG Pocket**, and the rise of **block puzzle** megahits like Block Blast!.

**📌 The pattern Rogers predicted:** technology keeps changing the rules. Touchscreens created new input, app stores created new distribution, F2P created new business models, and privacy rules (ATT) created new marketing realities. **Adapt or get left behind** describes mobile's whole history.

**🎮 Book examples (whole history section):** OXO, Spacewar!, Colossal Cave (lab era); Computer Space (first arcade game); Asteroids, Battlezone, Star Castle (vector); Pac-Man, Donkey Kong (raster icons); Tail Gunner, Star Trek, Out Run (elaborate cabinets); BattleTech Centers (peak arcade spectacle); Toy Story Midway Mania (dark-ride game that came home on the Wii); Vectrex (all-in-one exception); Auto Race, Game & Watch, Microvision, Game Boy + Tetris, DS, PSP, Mattel Football (handhelds); PC text adventures, sims, FPS, MMOs.

**🪝 Make it stick:** **"Lab → Bar → Arcade → Couch → Pocket."** Picture Pac-Man's evolution: born in a bar, partying in an arcade, napping on a couch, and now living in your pocket, asking whether you want 5 more moves for 900 coins.

## 4. Game genres
**🧠 Big idea:** A **genre** describes the **style of gameplay** (what you *do*), not the setting. Genres keep splitting into subgenres and merging into hybrids. Knowing them gives you the shared vocabulary for pitches, teardowns and market research.

**📋 The details:** gaming has splintered into many genres and subgenres over the years. Below is **every genre and subgenre in the book**, with its defining trait, the book's examples, and (added) where it lives on mobile today.

### 4a. Every book genre and subgenre

#### 🥊 Action: games that need hand-eye coordination
| Subgenre | Defining trait | Book examples | ➕ Mobile descendant |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Action-adventure** | Mix of genres: **item collection and use, puzzle solving, long-term story goals** | Prince of Persia series, Tomb Raider series | Open-world action RPGs like Genshin Impact (exploration, puzzles, story) |
| **Action-arcade** | Early-arcade style: **"twitch" gameplay, scoring, short play time** | Dig Dug, Diner Dash | Hyper-casual arcade games (Helix Jump); time-management games descended from Diner Dash |
| **Platformer** | A **mascot** jumps (or swings or bounces) through challenging "platform" environments, sometimes with shooting and fighting. **Once the most popular subgenre** | Mario titles: Super Mario World, Mario 64, Super Mario Galaxy | **Endless runners** (Temple Run, Subway Surfers) and one-touch auto-runners (Super Mario Run). Touch made the runner the mobile platformer |
| **Stealth** | Emphasis on **avoiding** enemies instead of fighting them | Metal Gear series, Thief: The Dark Project | Rare on mobile. Shows up as turn-based stealth puzzles (e.g. Hitman GO) or stealth levels inside hybrids |
| **Fighting** | Two or more opponents battle in **arenas**. Set apart from action games by the **depth of the controls** | Street Fighter series, Mortal Kombat series | Simplified tap/swipe fighters like Marvel Contest of Champions; depth moves from controls into **collection and upgrades** |
| **Beat 'em up / hack 'n' slash** | Fight **wave after wave** of enemies that get harder | Double Dragon, Castle Crashers | Roguelite wave-survival games (Archero, Survivor.io) with auto-attack and upgrade picks |

#### 🔫 Shooter: firing projectiles at enemies (fast, "twitch"). Subgenres are defined by the **camera**
| Subgenre | Defining trait | Book examples | ➕ Mobile descendant |
|---|---|---|---|
| **First-person shooter (FPS)** | Seen from the player's own eyes. A **tighter view: more limiting, but more personal** than third person | Quake, Team Fortress 2 | Call of Duty: Mobile (virtual sticks, aim assist, auto-fire options) |
| **Shoot 'em up ("shmup")** | Arcade-style. Shoot **huge numbers of enemies while dodging hazards**. Your avatar is usually a **vehicle** (like a spaceship). Many camera angles | Space Invaders, Contra series | "Survivor-like" auto-shooters (Survivor.io), dodge-and-shoot roguelites (Archero) |
| **Third-person shooter (TPS)** | Camera sits **behind** the player, showing some or all of the character and surroundings. The focus is still shooting | Star Wars Battlefront series, Grand Theft Auto series | Battle royales played in third person by default (PUBG Mobile, Free Fire) |

#### 🗝️ Adventure: puzzle solving, item collection and inventory management (the earliest were text-only)
Book examples for the umbrella genre: **Colossal Cave, King's Quest series, Leisure Suit Larry series.**

| Subgenre | Defining trait | Book examples | ➕ Mobile descendant |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Graphical adventure** | Use a **mouse or cursor to click** to uncover clues and move around | Myst, Monkey Island, Sam and Max series | Point-and-click and hidden-object adventures; touch replaces the mouse naturally |
| **Role-playing game (RPG)** | Based on **pen-and-paper RPGs** like Dungeons & Dragons. Choose a **character class**, raise **stats** through **combat, exploration and treasure**. Characters can be specific characters or generic classes | Star Wars: Knights of the Old Republic, Mass Effect series | **Gacha / hero-collection RPGs** (Raid: Shadow Legends, AFK Arena/AFK Journey, Honkai: Star Rail): stats, classes and gear grinding become the meta |
| **MMORPG** | An RPG supporting **hundreds of players in one world**. Known for **PvP**, repetitive **"grinding,"** and group battles called **"raids"** | World of Warcraft, DC Universe Online | Mobile MMORPGs (big in Asia); MMO DNA lives on in **clans/alliances, raid bosses and guild wars** across F2P |
| **Survival/horror** | Survive a horror scenario with **limited resources** (e.g. scarce ammo) | Resident Evil series, Silent Hill series | Survival-themed base builders and 4X (Whiteout Survival); the scarcity → tension mechanic powers many energy and resource loops |

> Note: the book files RPG, MMORPG and survival/horror under Adventure. Most people today treat RPG as a top-level genre. Learn the book's map, but expect modern market reports to group things differently.

#### 🏗️ Construction/management
- **Defining trait:** **build and expand** a location with **limited resources**. Can be **story-based or "toy"-based** (open-ended sandbox).
- **Book examples:** SimCity, Zoo Tycoon.
- ➕ **Mobile:** farm and town builders (Hay Day, Township), SimCity BuildIt, and the base-building layer of strategy games (Clash of Clans). Timers plus limited resources became a core F2P monetization rhythm.

#### 💕 Life simulation
- **Defining trait:** like management, but about **building and nurturing relationships with artificial life forms**.
- **Book examples:** The Sims, Princess Maker.
- **Pet simulation** subgenre: based on **Tamagotchi** digital-pet toys (now often much expanded). **Nurture animals by feeding them and building a relationship.** Example: **World of Zoo**.
- ➕ **Mobile:** The Sims FreePlay / The Sims Mobile, virtual-pet apps like My Talking Tom. The Tamagotchi "come back and care for me" loop is the ancestor of **daily check-ins and streaks**.

#### 🎵 Music/rhythm
- **Defining trait:** **match a rhythm or beat** to score points.
- **Range:** from simple (**Simon**) to complex (**Rock Band**).
- ➕ **Mobile:** tap-the-tiles rhythm games (Piano Tiles style), Beatstar. Often monetized through **song unlocks** and ads.

#### 🎉 Party
- **Defining trait:** designed for **multiple players**, built on **competition**, usually in **minigame** format.
- **Book examples:** Mario Party, Buzz!
- ➕ **Mobile:** Stumble Guys (obstacle-course party royale), Among Us (social deduction), rotating minigame events in casual games.

#### 🧩 Puzzle
- **Defining trait:** based on **logic and pattern completion**. Can be **slow and methodical** or need **hand-eye coordination**.
- **Book examples:** The Incredible Machine, Tetris.
- ➕ **Mobile:** the biggest casual category. **Match-3** (Candy Crush Saga, Royal Match), **blast/collapse** (Toon Blast), connect-the-dots (Two Dots), **block puzzle** (Block Blast!), physics puzzles (Cut the Rope), **3D match/sort** (Match Factory!). Usually paired with a **meta layer** (decorating, renovating, story) to give long-term goals.

#### ⚽ Sports
- **Defining trait:** based on **athletic competition**, traditional or extreme. **Annual versions** are common.
- **Book examples:** Madden series, Tony Hawk series.
- **Sports management** subgenre: instead of playing, you **manage players or teams**. Examples: FIFA Manager series, NFL Head Coach series.
- ➕ **Mobile:** EA SPORTS FC Mobile (card-collection team building), 8 Ball Pool, Golf Clash (short PvP sessions), football-manager games like Top Eleven. **Live seasons replace annual releases.**

#### ♟️ Strategy: thinking and planning, in historical or fictional settings
Book examples for the umbrella genre: **chess**, **Sid Meier's Civilization**.

| Subgenre | Defining trait | Book examples | ➕ Mobile descendant |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Real-time strategy (RTS)** | Faster than turn-based. Focus on the **"four X's": expansion, exploration, exploitation, extermination**. In 2010, **the dominant strategy subgenre** | Command & Conquer series, Dawn of War series | **Mobile 4X** (Rise of Kingdoms, Whiteout Survival, Last War: Survival) uses the four X's on a persistent map with timers; Clash Royale distills real-time strategy into 3-minute card battles; **MOBAs** (Mobile Legends: Bang Bang) grew from an RTS mod |
| **Turn-based** | Slower pace gives you **time to think**, so there's **more room for strategy** | X-Com series, Advance Wars series | Fire Emblem Heroes, turn-based gacha battles, card battlers (Marvel Snap, with simultaneous turns) |
| **Tower defense** | **Relatively new** (in 2010) on PC and handheld. Build automated, projectile-firing **towers** that hold off enemies | Defense Grid: The Awakening, Lock's Quest | A natural fit for touch: Kingdom Rush and Bloons TD (premium), PvP tower defense like Rush Royale |

> ⚠️ **Mind the term:** the book applies the "four X's" to RTS. Today "**4X**" usually means grand-strategy and empire games (Civilization-style), and on mobile it means the **persistent-map alliance-war genre**. Same four verbs, different packaging.

#### 🏎️ Vehicle simulation: piloting or driving a vehicle (sports car to spaceship), aiming to make it feel as **"real"** as possible
Book examples for the umbrella genre: **Lunar Lander, Densha de Go! 64** (a train-driving sim).

| Subgenre | Defining trait | Book examples | ➕ Mobile descendant |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Driving** | **Race and upgrade** vehicles (motorcycles to hovercraft). Can be **ultra-realistic or action-oriented** | Gran Turismo series, NASCAR Racing series, Wave Race, SSX | Arcade racers with car collection and upgrades (Asphalt series, CSR Racing), physics drivers (Hill Climb Racing), Mario Kart Tour |
| **Flying** | Fly for the **pleasure of flying** or into **combat**, or even **into space** | Microsoft Flight Simulator series (pleasure); Ace Combat, Blazing Angels (combat); Starfox, X-Wing/TIE Fighter (space) | Niche on mobile; mostly casual flight and plane-combat games |

### 4b. Hybrids and "other" classifications
- The list only **scratches the surface**. **Adult games, serious games, advert games ("advergames") and vehicular combat** are other classifications that cut across several genres.
- **Hybrids keep creating new genres:**
  - **Grand Theft Auto** combines action-adventure + third-person shooter + driving + life simulation + action-arcade in one game.
  - **Tuper Tario Tros.** (Swing Swing Submarine, a free browser game on Newgrounds) **fuses Super Mario Bros. with Tetris**.
- Rogers' challenge: what will the most popular genre of the future be? Maybe **you'll invent it**.

**🎮 Book examples:** every example is listed in the tables above. GTA and Tuper Tario Tros. are the hybrid showcases.

**📱 F2P mobile translation: hybrids are how mobile hits are born.** A few that worked:
- **Match-3 + home decoration + story** (Homescapes/Gardenscapes): puzzle as the core loop, renovation as the meta.
- **Board game + slot-like dice rolls + social events** (Monopoly GO!).
- **Tower defense + card collection + PvP** (Clash Royale: towers, a card deck, real-time duels).
- **Roguelite + shooter + one-thumb control** (Archero).
- **Survivor-like action + idle progression + gacha** (Survivor.io and its many followers).
- **Hybrid-casual** itself is a business-model hybrid: hyper-casual simplicity plus mid-core meta and IAP.
- **Advergames** became **playable ads**. The interactive mini-game ad is now a core UA tool for mobile games.

**🪝 Make it stick:** genres are **LEGO bricks**, not museum labels. GTA is a five-brick tower and Tuper Tario Tros. is a Mario brick jammed into a Tetris brick. Your job is to find the combination nobody has snapped together yet.

### 4c. ➕ The mobile genre map (2026)

## 5. Who makes this stuff?
**🧠 Big idea:** Games went from **one-person projects** to **film-crew-sized teams of specialists**. Each discipline keeps splitting into narrower jobs as games get bigger, more complex and longer to make.

**📋 The details: how teams evolved**
1. **Solo era:** early games were made by individuals. The original **Prince of Persia** was made by **one person, Jordan Mechner**, who programmed, designed and animated it, and even **composed the music**.
2. **Programmer teams:** as commercial development got serious, games needed **two or three programmers**.
3. **Artists join:** players started demanding **better-looking games**.
4. **Design by whoever had the idea:** at first, design was done by **whichever team member had the good idea**.
5. **Dedicated designers:** once game content became **too involved for one person** to handle on the side, a **dedicated design position** was created.
6. **Specialization today:** people still **wear many hats**, but **specialization is increasingly necessary** as games grow.
- Teams that make games are called **developers** or **development teams**. They're like a **movie or TV production team**: lots of creative people working together to make entertainment.

**📱 F2P mobile translation:** mobile repeated this history at high speed. Early App Store hits often came from **tiny teams** (one to a handful of people). Today's top-grossing live games are run by **teams of dozens to hundreds**, with new specializations the book never imagined (LiveOps, data, UA, monetization; see section 7). **Hyper-casual and indie mobile** still has the "wear many hats" model, where a designer might also build the prototype, make the ad and read the dashboard.

**🪝 Make it stick:** Jordan Mechner is a one-man band (drums, guitar, harmonica, and he animates the dancers). Modern AAA and F2P teams are a full orchestra with a conductor (producer) and a composer (designer) arguing about the tempo.

### 5.1 Programmer
**🧠 Big idea:** programmers turn the design into a working game: rendering, controls, camera, physics, AI, scripting, tools and more.

**📋 The details:**
- Uses languages like **C++ and Java** to write code that:
  - **displays** the game's text and graphics,
  - runs the **control systems** that let the player interact,
  - builds the **camera system** that lets the player see the world,
  - programs the **physics system** affecting the player and the world,
  - writes the **AI system** controlling enemies, plus **object scripting**, and the list goes on.
- **Specializations:**
  - **Tools programmers** build tools that help the team make the game more efficiently.
  - **Physics/simulation programmers** simulate real-world physics (like **realistic water**) or develop **inverse kinematics** for characters (the book prints "inverse kinetics").
  - **Audio/sound-tools programmers** build tools to play music and effects.
- **Required knowledge** (whatever the specialty): strong **math**, **2-D/3-D graphics**, **physics**, **particle effects**, **UI**, **AI**, **input devices** and **networking**.
- These skills are **always in demand**. Some programmers work as **contractors ("hired guns")**, moving project to project and writing code or temporary fixes for struggling teams.
- *(Visual: a programmer in sunglasses with a keyboard under one arm and a coffee mug with a "?" on it in the other.)*

**🎮 Book examples:** realistic water and inverse kinematics as specialist work, and tools programming as a force multiplier.

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- **Client programmers** (gameplay, UI, performance on **low-end Android devices**, battery and memory budgets).
- **Server/backend programmers**: player accounts, inventories and economies kept **server-side** to stop cheating, matchmaking, events, leaderboards, cross-device saves.
- **Tools/LiveOps tooling**: config dashboards so designers can launch events, offers and A/B tests **without a new app release**. In live games this is often the biggest productivity multiplier, the modern version of Rogers' tools programmer.
- **SDK integration**: analytics, ad networks, attribution, payments, push notifications.

> ⚠️ **2010 → 2026:** C++ still powers engines, but most mobile game code today is **C# in Unity**. Other common stacks: Unreal (C++) for high-end, **Cocos Creator (TypeScript)** popular for casual and **playable ads**, plus Kotlin and Swift for native platform work.

**🪝 Make it stick:** the programmer is the game's **plumber, electrician and physicist** in one. If the water looks real and the hero's knees bend right, thank them.

### 5.2 Artist
**🧠 Big idea:** art went from blocky "programmer art" to specialized pipelines (concept, modeling, texturing, VFX, UI, animation, tech art) under an art director who guards the visual vision.

**📋 The details:**
- **Origins:** in the early days programmers made all the art. It was so blocky that placeholder art is still called **"programmer art."** (Footnote: Rogers apologizes to programmers, but says he didn't invent the term.)
- **Shigeru Miyamoto** was one of the first real artists in games. He created **Mario and Donkey Kong**, memorable cartoon characters drawn with an **8-bit CPU and 2-bit pixels**: a lot of **personality per pixel**.
- **Rare early exceptions:** **Dragon's Lair** (Cinematronics, 1983) and **Space Ace** (Cinematronics, 1984), beautifully animated by **ex-Disney animators like Don Bluth**. They were rare because they used **laserdiscs** to play video footage.
- **Better hardware** (more memory, color depth, bigger sprites) allowed more detailed images, backgrounds and characters, like the hand-drawn, hand-animated **Darkstalkers** (Capcom, 1994) and **Metal Slug** (SNK, 1996).
- **The 3-D shift:**
  - 3-D graphics were once limited to movies like **Tron** (Disney, 1982) and Pixar shorts like **Luxo Jr.** (1986), until high-end software became affordable.
  - **Pre-rendered 3-D** in games: **Myst** (Broderbund, 1993), **Donkey Kong Country** (Nintendo, 1994).
  - **True 3-D** existed in arcades as early as **Battlezone** (Atari, 1980), but the **PlayStation (1994)** inspired developers to build realistic 3-D worlds and characters for home consoles.
- **Art specializations:**
  - **Concept artist**: draws characters, worlds and enemies using traditional media and computers.
  - **Storyboard artist**: illustrates cinematics and sometimes parts of the gameplay design, for other artists and animators.
  - **3-D modelers and environment artists**: build characters and environments in programs like **Maya and 3D Studio Max**.
  - **Texture artists**: paint the surface detail (the "skin") onto 3-D models and locations.
  - **Visual effects (VFX) artists**: create spectacular effects with 2-D and 3-D art.
  - **User interface (UI) artist**: designs the **icons and elements** of the interface and **HUD**.
  - **Animators**: animate the player character and create **cutscenes**, just like in big animated films.
  - **Technical artists**: help every artist, e.g. **rigging** models so animators can move them, and teaching new tools and tech.
  - **Art director**: supervises all artists and maintains the **artistic vision** for the whole project.
- **Advice for any art role:** study the **basics** and **keep drawing**.
- *(Visual: a stern, bearded artist in black, holding a pencil up like a torch.)*

**🎮 Book examples:** Miyamoto (personality per pixel), Dragon's Lair and Space Ace (laserdisc animation), Darkstalkers and Metal Slug (hand-drawn sprite peak), Tron and Luxo Jr. (3-D in film), Myst and DKC (pre-rendered), Battlezone (early true 3-D), PlayStation (the 3-D push).

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- **"Personality per pixel" is still the rule.** Characters and icons must read at **thumbnail size** on a phone screen and in a store icon. Miyamoto's constraint is your constraint.
- **UI art is huge in F2P.** The shop, offers, event banners, currency icons, reward chests and progress bars are where players look all the time. UI/UX artists are central, not an afterthought.
- **Live art pipelines:** a new event theme, skins and banners every few weeks. Art has to be **fast, reusable and modular**.
- **Marketing and UA art:** store screenshots, icons, video ads and **playable ads** are made constantly and A/B tested. The icon alone can noticeably change conversion on the store page.
- **Performance budgets:** texture sizes, draw calls and download size all matter on low-end devices, and technical artists own a lot of that.

**🪝 Make it stick:** Mario was drawn with fewer pixels than your phone's battery icon, and he's still the most famous character in games. **Personality beats resolution.**

### 5.3 Designer
**🧠 Big idea:** whatever the title, the designer **creates the ideas and rules that make up a game**, can explain **why** a game is good or bad, and is ultimately responsible for whether it's **fun**.

**📋 The details:**
- **Many titles, same core role:** director, planner, producer, lead designer, senior game designer. (Footnote: a producer's job involves more than design, and sometimes a producer doesn't design at all. More in 5.4.)
- **The role:** create the **ideas and rules** of the game.
- **Skills:** many, many. **Loving to play games** is just the baseline.
  - Footnote: Rogers endorses **Jesse Schell**'s list (*The Art of Game Design*, 2008) and calls it pretty accurate. It names **19 fields** a well-rounded designer should understand. Grouped for memory:
    - **Arts and media:** animation, cinematography, music, sound design, visual arts.
    - **People and ideas:** anthropology, history, psychology, communication, public speaking.
    - **Words and thinking:** brainstorming, creative writing, technical writing.
    - **Build and business:** architecture, business, economics, engineering, management, mathematics.
- **Critical skill:** tell a **good game from a bad one** and, even more important, **explain why**. Saying it's bad because "it sucks" is **never** acceptable feedback.
- **Design specializations:**
  - **Level designers**: make **paper maps**, build **"grey box"** worlds in 3-D programs, and **populate** levels with everything from enemies to treasure.
  - **System designers**: define **how game elements relate to each other**, like the **economy** or a **technology tree**.
  - **Scripters**: use tools to write code that makes things happen in-game, from **springing a trap** to **choreographing a camera move**.
  - **Combat designers**: focus on **fights between the player and enemies**, and on **tuning** how hard, fair and satisfying those fights feel (the book calls this "balancing").
  - **Creative director**: maintains the **vision** of the game, supervises the other designers and suggests improvements.
- **One more responsibility:** making sure the game is **"fun."** Rogers deliberately leaves that can of worms closed until later in the book.
- *(Visual: a thoughtful designer stroking his chin, lightbulb overhead, a "GDD" tucked under his arm.)*

**🎮 Book examples:** no specific games. The examples are the specialist jobs: grey-boxing, economies, tech trees, trap and camera scripting, combat balancing.

**📱 F2P mobile translation (full breakdown in section 7):**
- The book's **system designer** has split into **systems designers** (progression, combat formulas, upgrade trees) and **economy designers** (currencies, **sources and sinks**, reward tables, pricing logic).
- **Level designers** on puzzle games may ship **new levels every week** and live in per-level **win-rate and attempts data**.
- **Scripters** → **technical designers** who configure events, quests and offers in data tools.
- **Combat designers** → **balance designers** for PvP and gacha (hero stats, meta health).
- **New jobs:** **LiveOps designer**, **monetization designer**, **UX designer**, **narrative designer**.
- **"Explain why" in F2P** means **evidence**: "Level 47 has a 12% first-try win rate versus our 35% target, and churn spikes there" beats "Level 47 sucks." (Hypothetical numbers.) Data doesn't replace taste. It lets you **argue** for your taste.

**🪝 Make it stick:** the designer is a **rule-writing chef**. Anyone can say the soup is bad, but the chef says "too much salt, not enough acid, and it needs 10 minutes less." Never serve "it sucks" as feedback.

### 5.4 Producer
**🧠 Big idea:** the producer **oversees the whole team** and keeps every moving part (people, money, schedule, partners) working together. How much creative power they have depends heavily on the studio.

**📋 The details:**
- **Origins:** producers were originally **designers who also managed** their team's work. The role has **expanded dramatically**.
- **Responsibilities (nine of them):**
  - **People:** **hiring and building** the team, and **resolving disputes** between creative leads and programming leads
  - **Paper and money:** **drawing up contracts**, **balancing the budget**, **managing the work schedule**
  - **Creative input:** **contributing to the game's design**
  - **Representation:** acting as the team's voice to upper management and publishers
  - **Outside resources:** **coordinating** art, music and cutscenes made outside the team
  - **Quality and reach:** **arranging testing and localization**
- Producers are usually **first on and last off** a production.
- They're often the **public face** of the game, talking to press and the public. Footnote: that's because they're the one person who can keep all the moving parts straight.
- **Assistant and associate producers** help with the day-to-day. Even tasks that seem "trivial" or "menial," like **ordering dinner for a team working late**, can be among the most important things a producer does for the team.
- **It varies wildly:** some studios think producers are **unnecessary**. Others want them to have **no creative control**, only schedule and production.
- *(Visual: a frazzled producer yelling into a phone with one finger raised ("one sec!"), with a foot planted on a squashed little face. The pressure has to land somewhere.)*

**🎮 Book examples:** no games. The vivid detail is the late-night dinner order as real team care.

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- In live games, the producer runs the **release train** (an update every few weeks), the **LiveOps calendar**, cross-team dependencies, and coordination with **platform featuring** and partners.
- Many mobile studios split the old producer role:
  - **Producer / project manager**: schedule, process, risk.
  - **Product manager**: KPIs, roadmap, priorities (section 7).
  - **Game director**: creative vision.
- The "ordering dinner" lesson still holds. **Protecting the team** from chaos and burnout is one of the highest-leverage things a producer does, especially in never-ending live service.

**🪝 Make it stick:** the producer is a **juggler on a unicycle**: schedule, budget, people and publisher in the air at once, and they still remember who's vegetarian when ordering pizza at 11 pm.

### 5.5 Tester (QA)
**🧠 Big idea:** testers play the same content over and over to find and report **bugs**. That unglamorous work is what stops games from shipping broken. It's also a classic **entry point** into the industry.

**📋 The details:**
- **The pitch:** do you like playing the same level over and over, again and again? Testing is for you.
- **The reality:** long hours, cramped workspaces, play that many would find mind-numbingly boring.
- **The skills (more than you'd think):** **patience, persistence and great communication**, to report problems (**"bugs"**) clearly.
- **Without testers:** games that **crash on loading**, **bad cameras**, **broken combat** and **unfair difficulty**.
- **Quality assurance (QA)** (footnote: a fancy name for the test department) is crucial. **Publishers hold games to rigorous quality standards**, so what you buy is (mostly) bug-free. Meeting that standard takes **weeks, if not months**, of testing. A game is ready for release only after it **passes QA**.
- **Gateway job:** Rogers has seen testers become **designers, artists, producers and even studio heads**. You learn a lot about games fast.
- **Testers prevent games from sucking.** Remember that before you make fun of one.
- *(Visual: a worn-out tester hunched forward in an office chair, gripping a controller, slack-jawed. That's hour nine of the same level.)*

**🎮 Book examples:** no games. The failure list (crashes, bad cameras, broken combat, unfair difficulty) is the evidence.

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- **Device fragmentation:** thousands of Android models, OS versions, screen notches and aspect ratios. Mobile QA tests across **device matrices** and cloud device farms.
- **Live QA never ends:** every update and **every event config** (prices, rewards, start and end times, time zones) must be checked. A misconfigured offer can give away premium currency or overcharge players.
- **Store compliance:** app store review rules, age ratings, privacy and loot-box odds disclosure.
- **Playtesting and user research** sit next to QA. Bug testing asks "does it work?", and playtesting asks "is it clear and fun?"
- **Still a gateway job:** QA → level design, QA → LiveOps and QA → production are common paths in mobile studios.

**🪝 Make it stick:** the tester is the **goalkeeper**. Nobody notices 1,000 saves, everyone notices the one goal. Respect the keeper.

### 5.6 Composer
**🧠 Big idea:** music is **hugely important** to how a game feels. Game composers write music that is short or loops seamlessly, and that is **distinct and fits the game**.

**📋 The details:**
- Early game music was crude **beeps and bloops**, yet people can still hum the **Legend of Zelda** and **Super Mario Bros.** themes.
- The **composer** creates the game's music, usually on a **keyboard or synthesizer** that can imitate any instrument.
- As sound tech improved, many composers began writing **live and orchestral** scores, which needs extra skills like **conducting an orchestra** (Rogers admits he has no idea what the baton-waving means).
- **Home audio software** can now mix and master **professional-sounding samples**.
- **How to break in:** **write music, record it, and get your samples to a game producer.**
- **How hiring works (from Rogers, who has reviewed many audio résumés):** the designer has a **specific style or feel** in mind. If your sample **matches** it, they contact you. The deciding factor is **originality plus fit**: music that is distinctive and serves what this particular game needs.
- **Danny Elfman** example: his very distinct scores for **Beetlejuice** and **Pee-wee's Big Adventure** made everyone want "his sound" in their movies. A distinct style becomes a demand.
- **Games ≠ movies:** most game themes are **very short** or must **repeat over and over**. Writing powerful, exciting music within those limits beats just writing "songs."
  - Footnote: songwriters shouldn't despair. Plenty of games still use traditional songs, especially **sports and rhythm games**.
- More on music in **Level 15** (→ L15).

**🎮 Book examples:** Zelda and Mario themes (memorable despite primitive tech), Danny Elfman (Beetlejuice, Pee-wee: a distinct style creates demand).

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- **Many mobile players play muted** (commuting, in bed, in public). So never put **critical information only in audio**: back every audio cue with a visual one. Music still matters a lot for **brand and feel**, as an instantly recognizable menu theme proves.
- **Loops are life:** players sit on the map or home screen for hours across many sessions. Loops must **not get annoying**, so vary them by season and event.
- **Download size** limits audio quality and quantity. Stream or download event music later.
- **LiveOps music:** themed tracks for seasons, holidays and events keep the game feeling fresh.

**🪝 Make it stick:** game music is a **jingle that has to survive 1,000 replays without making you throw your phone**. Zelda pulled it off with beeps.

### 5.7 Sound designer
**🧠 Big idea:** the sound designer creates **all the sound effects**, and those sounds **carry gameplay information**: "good!", "danger!", "treasure!"

**📋 The details:**
- **Composer = music. Sound designer = sound effects.**
- **Try this:** play a game with the sound off. It isn't the same. A lot of **information reaches the player through sound**, and these **audio cues** are the sound designer's job.
- Rogers thinks sound design is a lot of fun. **Games come alive once sound is added**, which is why even **placeholder sound effects** matter during development.
- **Mixing and blending** sounds into something nobody has heard before is part of the craft.
- **A good sound designer understands the game** and makes sounds that **help the player**:
  - **"Positive" sounds** confirm you're doing something right or collecting something good.
  - **Warning sounds** signal danger or a possibly bad choice.
  - A sound can be made **happy, deadly, scary, or like a big pile of treasure**, sometimes all at once.
- **Taking direction:** you'll get directions from people who **may not know what they want**. Rogers' real example (footnote: an actual direction, and the sound designer still delivered something great): a creature should sound like a phlegmy cougar from hell, but more shrieky than growly.

**🎮 Book examples:** the "phlegmy hellcougar" direction, a real request that shows vague creative direction is part of the job.

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- **Reward sounds are core to F2P feel:** the coin cascade, the chest-opening build-up, the level-complete fanfare, combo escalations in match-3. **Juice = sound + VFX + haptics.**
- **Haptics** (vibration) is a sound designer's second channel on phones, and it still works when the sound is off.
- **Purchase and reward confirmation sounds** reassure players. **Warning sounds** (a timer running out, a last-move alert) build tension, but use them ethically, not to pressure spending.
- **Placeholder audio in prototypes** makes a huge difference in playtests and in ad creatives.

**🪝 Make it stick:** close your eyes and imagine opening a legendary chest **in total silence**. Sad, right? The sound designer makes the confetti *audible*.

### 5.8 Writer
**🧠 Big idea:** unlike in Hollywood, game writers are **usually hired late** and freelance. They fix stories, write dialogue, clarify instructions, write support material, and sometimes add name value. Writing *for games* is its own craft.

**📋 The details:**
- **Not the "idea person":** in Hollywood writers create the initial idea. In games, writers come in **late in production**. If you want to be the idea person, **go into game design**.
- Writers do contribute, but it's **usually not a full-time team position**. They're typically **freelancers** brought in for one of these reasons:
  1. **Rewriting the story** once the team realizes its own story is drivel.
  2. **Writing dialogue** for characters and cutscenes, once the team realizes good dialogue is hard.
  3. **Making things clearer**, e.g. **instructional or directional prompts**.
  4. **Writing the manual and fiction support material**, such as character bios on the publisher's website.
  5. **Adding "star power"** to the back of the box. This goes in and out of fashion depending on how much the industry values "name writers" at the moment.
- **Upside:** there's usually **plenty of work** if you don't mind varied writing jobs for different companies.
- **Requirements:** know how to write, use proper grammar, and know **screenplay format**. Most important of all: know **how to write for video games**, which is very different from books or movies. **Level 3** covers it (→ L03).

**🎮 Book examples:** no games. The five reasons are the content.

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- Mobile writing is mostly **narrative design plus UX writing**: event storylines, character bios for gacha heroes, quest text, **UI microcopy** (button labels, error messages), **push notification copy**, **store descriptions**, and offer names.
- **Reason #3 (clarity prompts) is the big one on mobile.** Players skim, so every tutorial line must be short and instantly clear. That's often the difference between a good and bad **FTUE**.
- **Localization:** text goes out in many languages, so writers must keep strings short, avoid untranslatable puns, and leave room for text expansion in UI.
- In gacha and story-heavy games, writers are often **full-time**, because character lore drives desire for characters and spending.

> ⚠️ **2010 → 2026:** "Writers are freelancers hired late" is less true now. Narrative-heavy live games (gacha RPGs, story-driven puzzle metas) employ in-house narrative teams, because story is part of the LiveOps content treadmill.

**🪝 Make it stick:** Hollywood writer = architect who draws the house. 2010 game writer = interior decorator called in after the walls are up ("please make this make sense"). 2026 narrative designer = the one who keeps adding rooms every season.

## 6. Have you thought about publishing?
**🧠 Big idea:** there's a **second career path** most people don't know about. **Publishers** fund, oversee, legally protect, manufacture, market and distribute games, and they employ designers, artists and programmers in oversight roles.

**📋 The details: what publishers do**
- **Fund** development teams.
- **Manage** the game's production.
- **Handle legal issues.**
- **Manufacture** the game.
- **Public relations and marketing.**
- **Distribution** of the finished product.

### 6.1 Product manager
- Like a game producer, but on the publisher side. **Manages the development team against the agreed production schedule.**
- **Duties:**
  - Helps set **production priorities**.
  - Acts as the **go-between for the studio and the publisher's legal department**.
  - **Reviews and approves milestones.**
  - **Makes payments to the studio** (usually tied to milestones).
  - Works with the **ESRB** to secure the game's **rating**. Footnote: the ESRB (Entertainment Software Rating Board) rates games in the US.
- **Influence varies:** at some publishers the product manager has **extensive say over content**. At others they just keep development running smoothly. Rogers is glad he isn't the one making the schedule.

### 6.2 Creative manager (Rogers' own job at THQ)
- Rogers calls it the job people picture when they imagine working in games. In truth it isn't *just* dreaming up and playing games all day… though sometimes it really is.
- The role is typically filled by **designers or writers who moved to the publishing side**. Like product managers, their involvement **varies by publisher**.
- **What Rogers has done in the role:**
  - Worked with teams to **create and develop** games.
  - **Written game pitches.**
  - Worked with **licensors** to create game concepts.
  - **Played game builds** (footnote: a **"build"** or **"burn"** is an in-progress version of the game, playable on a computer or a special console) to make sure they **stay true to the core idea and are "fun."**
- **The key value: the "thousand-foot view."** Like looking down on the game from a thousand feet up (not looking at thousands of feet!), it's an **unbiased outside viewpoint** that helps root out **weaknesses in the design and construction**.
  - When things aren't solid, the creative manager gives the team **clear feedback** on how to improve gameplay, or advice on **exploring another creative direction**.
- Also works with **marketing and PR** on **press materials** so the game is shown at its best.

### 6.3 Art director (publishing side)
- Like a creative manager, but **only for art**.
- Helps a team **create a visual style**, and pushes the art in **directions the team hadn't considered**.
- Helps **"globalize" the game's visual language** so it's **clear to the player** (consistent meaning of colors, shapes and icons across the whole game).
- Works with **marketing** on **packaging** (e.g. the **box cover**) and gathers the **assets needed to publicize** the game.

### 6.4 Technical director (publishing side)
- Comes from a **programming background**.
- **Reviews and recommends tools and software** to help teams work more efficiently.
- Provides **technical support and advice** where a team's programming staff is weak.
- Performs **due diligence on new teams**: can this studio actually build the game it's being hired to make?

### 6.5 And the rest…
Publishing jobs that aren't directly about making games but matter for creating and selling them:
- **Business development**: builds relationships with studios, holds **pitch meetings**, reviews **prospective demos**, makes **deals with external studios**, and finds **emerging studios to acquire**. If you ever run a studio, you'll meet plenty of them.
- **Lawyer**: negotiates **all contracts** and makes sure the team isn't creating **content that gets the publisher into legal trouble**.
- **Brand manager**: creates the **marketing strategy** to promote and advertise the game, and develops **print material** such as manuals and box covers.
- **Public relations (PR) manager**: handles the press, meaning **gaming magazines** and **press events** that present the game well.
- **Quality assurance (QA) manager**: runs the **test department**, organizing and relaying **bug sheets** back to the developer.

### 6.6 The wider ecosystem
People outside production and publishing who work with both:
- **Talent recruiter**: finds new talent and helps place people with developers and publishers.
- **Game reviewers**: play games before release and write **reviews and interviews** for magazines and websites.
- **Licensors**: staff at big entertainment companies who guard how their **brands appear** in games built on their properties.

**Wrap-up:** there are plenty of career options in games, but Rogers says forget them all: **game design is where the real fun is.** And great games need **great ideas**, which is where Level 2 starts (→ L02).

**🎮 Book examples:** Rogers' own creative manager work at THQ (pitches, licensors, playing builds) and ESRB ratings. Everything else is role descriptions.

**📱 F2P mobile translation: what publishing looks like on mobile**
- **The app stores are the new distributors and retailers.** Apple's App Store and Google Play handle distribution and payment processing and take a revenue share (commonly 15–30% depending on program and revenue). **Manufacturing is gone**, since there are no discs or boxes.
- **Self-publishing is normal.** Many mobile studios publish their own games. **Mobile publishers** still exist, especially in hyper-casual and hybrid-casual, offering **UA money and expertise, analytics and live-ops know-how** in exchange for a revenue share. A common hyper-casual practice is the **CPI test**: publish a short ad for a prototype to see if people would click and install *before* building the full game.
- **Product manager means something different in mobile.** In the book, it's a publisher-side schedule, milestone and payments role. In a modern F2P studio, the **product manager** usually **owns the game's KPIs and roadmap** (retention, monetization, engagement), decides what to build next based on data and strategy, and runs A/B tests. It's closer to "CEO of the game's business" (section 7).
- **The creative manager's thousand-foot view survives** as **publishing producers or game leads** at publishers and platform partners, and as **internal greenlight reviews**: outside eyes play builds and read soft-launch KPIs to decide whether a game goes global, pivots or gets killed.
- **Art director → brand and creative teams:** the store icon, screenshots and **ad creatives** are the "box cover" now. These get A/B tested constantly, and **globalizing visual language** matters even more when players skim tiny screens across dozens of countries.
- **Technical director due diligence → tech audits** of SDKs, backend scalability, crash rates and build size.
- **Business development → publishing and partnership managers** who scout prototypes, negotiate IP licenses (e.g. brand collabs in live games) and arrange platform featuring.
- **Lawyer → legal and compliance:** privacy laws (e.g. GDPR in Europe, children's privacy rules like COPPA in the US), **loot-box regulations** and odds disclosure, consumer-protection rules on pricing and ads, and IP licenses for collab events.
- **Brand manager / PR → UA + marketing + community:** paid user acquisition, **ASO** (app store optimization), influencer and creator campaigns, and **community managers** on Discord and social media. Players are the press now.
- **Ratings:** the ESRB still rates console games in the US, but mobile stores use their own age-rating questionnaires (Google Play uses the **IARC** system, which maps to regional boards like ESRB and PEGI).
- **Reviewers → creators and influencers:** store ratings, YouTube and TikTok creators, and streamers shape mobile discovery more than magazines ever did.
- **Licensors → collab partners:** licensed collaborations (characters from films, anime or other games appearing as limited-time events) are a major LiveOps tool, and licensors approve how their IP looks.

> ⚠️ **2010 → 2026:** The book's model is **"developer makes it, publisher funds, boxes and sells it."** Mobile F2P is **"studio runs a live service, platforms distribute, UA buys players, data decides."** The *functions* (funding, oversight, legal, marketing, QA, outside creative review) all still exist. They've just been reorganized around **continuous operation** instead of a single launch day.

**🪝 Make it stick:** the publisher is the game's **record label**: it pays for the studio, signs the contracts, prints the album covers and gets you on the radio. On mobile, the radio is the app store, the album cover is your icon, and the airplay is paid UA.

## 7. ➕ The modern F2P mobile team (who does what in 2026)
**🧠 Big idea:** a live F2P game is a **service**, so its team adds roles for **operating** the game (LiveOps), **measuring** it (data), **monetizing** it (economy and offers) and **growing** it (UA). Knowing who owns what helps you pick your path and talk to everyone in their own language.

### 7.1 The design family, side by side
| Role | Owns | Day-to-day verbs | Typical metrics they watch | Book ancestor | Signature artifact |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Game designer** (generalist / feature designer) | Features, rules, player experience of a mode or system | Concept, spec, prototype, playtest, iterate | Feature engagement, funnel completion, playtest feedback | Designer | One-page feature spec with mockups and flow |
| **Systems designer** | How systems interlock: progression, combat formulas, upgrade trees, matchmaking rules | Model, simulate, balance | Progression speed, win rates, time-to-level | System designer / combat designer | Spreadsheet models, system diagrams |
| **Economy designer** | Currencies, **sources and sinks**, reward tables, prices of in-game items, inflation control | Balance flows, set reward values, model player wallets | Currency balance per player segment, sink usage, conversion to payer, progression pace by spender tier | System designer (economy) | Economy model: sources and sinks per day, per segment |
| **Level designer** (puzzle / campaign) | Individual levels and the **difficulty curve** | Build levels in editors, tune goals and moves, replace bad levels | Per-level win rate, attempts per level, churn per level, booster usage | Level designer | Level files + difficulty curve chart |
| **LiveOps designer** | The **event calendar**: tournaments, seasonal events, passes, limited-time modes | Plan the calendar, configure events, tune rewards, analyze results | Event participation, event revenue, retention lift, reactivation | *(none: designer + producer hybrid)* | LiveOps calendar + event templates |
| **Monetization designer** | Store, offers, bundles, starter packs, passes, ad placements | Design offers, price tests, segment targeting | Conversion rate, ARPDAU, ARPPU, offer take-rate, ad impressions per DAU | *(none: system design meets brand manager)* | Offer matrix + A/B test plan |
| **UX / UI designer** | Flows, FTUE, HUD, menus on small screens | Wireframe, prototype, usability test | Tutorial completion, time-to-first-action, drop-off points | UI artist + designer | Wireframes and clickable prototypes |
| **Narrative designer** | Story, characters, event narrative, UI text tone | Write, design story-delivery systems | Story-mode engagement, character popularity | Writer | Story bible, event scripts |
| **Technical designer / scripter** | Implementing designs in tools and config, quest and event scripting | Configure data, script logic, build tools | Bugs or misconfigs per release | Scripter | Config tables, scripts |
| **Game director / creative director** | The vision, the "is it fun?" call, priorities across design | Review, decide, defend the vision | Overall health: retention, sentiment, revenue | Creative director | Vision doc / pillars |

**🔑 The distinctions people mix up:**
- **Game designer vs systems designer:** the game designer asks *"what does the player do and feel?"* The systems designer asks *"how do all the numbers and rules connect so it stays balanced for months?"*
- **Systems vs economy designer:** systems covers *all* interlocking rules (combat, progression). Economy specifically covers **value flow**: what players earn, spend and buy, and whether the wallet inflates or drains.
- **Level designer vs LiveOps designer:** the level designer crafts **permanent content** (level 1 to level 10,000). The LiveOps designer crafts **temporary content and the rhythm of the calendar** (this weekend's tournament, next month's season).
- **Monetization designer vs economy designer:** the economy designer makes sure the game's value system is healthy for **all** players. The monetization designer designs **how and when value is offered for money**. Good studios make them work together so monetization never breaks the economy.
- **Game designer vs product manager:** the designer owns *what makes it fun and how it works*. The PM owns *why we build it now and what business goal it moves*. Healthy teams argue productively; unhealthy ones let one side win every argument.

### 7.2 The business and growth roles
| Role | Owns | Key metrics | Book ancestor |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Product manager (PM)** | Game KPIs, roadmap, prioritization, A/B testing strategy, business cases | D1/D7/D30 retention, ARPDAU, LTV, payer conversion, feature impact | Publisher's product manager + producer + creative manager's thousand-foot view |
| **Data analyst / data scientist** | Dashboards, funnels, cohort analysis, A/B test analysis, segmentation, forecasts | Statistical significance, cohort curves, predicted LTV | *(none: the tester's bug sheet grew into telemetry)* |
| **UA (user acquisition) manager** | Buying installs through ad networks and platforms, budget allocation, campaign optimization | **CPI** (cost per install), **ROAS** (return on ad spend), **LTV > CPI**, payback period | Brand manager / marketing |
| **Creative / ad production team** | Video ads, **playable ads**, store screenshots, icons | Click-through rate, install rate, creative fatigue | Advergames + art director + brand manager |
| **ASO / store manager** | Store page, keywords, featuring requests, ratings | Store conversion rate, keyword ranks | Brand manager / PR |
| **CRM / LiveOps manager** | Push notifications, inbox messages, segment-targeted offers, win-back campaigns | Reactivation rate, notification opt-in and open rates | PR manager + producer |
| **Community manager** | Discord, social channels, creators, player feedback loops | Sentiment, community size, response time | PR manager |
| **Player support** | Tickets, refunds, bug reports from players | Ticket volume, resolution time, satisfaction | QA + PR |

### 7.3 Production, engineering, art, QA (modern versions)
- **Producer / project manager**: release train, sprint planning, dependencies, risk. (Book: producer.)
- **Client engineers** (Unity/C#, Cocos/TypeScript, Unreal/C++), **server/backend engineers** (accounts, economy and anti-cheat on the server), **tools engineers** (LiveOps config tools), **SDK/ads engineers**. (Book: programmer.)
- **2D/3D artists, animators, VFX, UI artists, technical artists, marketing artists**. (Book: artist roles.)
- **QA**: device-matrix testing, release regression, **event config checks**, automation. (Book: tester.)
- **Audio**: often outsourced or a small team: music loops, SFX, haptics. (Book: composer, sound designer.)

### 7.4 How a feature flows through the team (a worked example)
**"Halloween Haunt" weekend event in a hypothetical match-3** (a labeled hypothetical):
1. **PM:** "D30 retention dipped among players past level 500. Let's target them with a mid-month event. Goal: +X% weekend sessions in that segment."
2. **LiveOps designer:** picks a proven event template (a collect-the-pumpkins race), places it on the calendar, and drafts reward tiers.
3. **Economy designer:** checks that rewards don't flood the economy (how many boosters and coins per player, compared with normal weekly income).
4. **Monetization designer:** adds an optional event bundle and a "finish the race" offer, priced and segmented, with an A/B test.
5. **Level designer:** builds 20 themed bonus levels with a gentler difficulty curve.
6. **UX/UI + artists:** event banner, progress bar, themed board skin, reward popup. **Narrative designer:** a 3-panel story intro.
7. **Engineers:** most of it is configured in the LiveOps tool with no app update. New mechanics would need a client release.
8. **QA:** tests configs in every time zone, checks reward amounts, verifies the end-of-event payout.
9. **CRM:** push notification and in-game inbox message to the target segment. **UA/creative:** a Halloween ad variant for new-player campaigns.
10. **Data analyst:** reads results (participation, retention lift, revenue, any cannibalization of regular spend) and writes the post-mortem.
11. **Game director:** "Was it *fun*? Did it fit the game's identity?" (Rogers' designer duty never goes away.)

### 7.5 Doors into F2P design (career map)
- **QA → level design / LiveOps** (the gateway Rogers describes still works).
- **Level designer (puzzle studio) → systems / LiveOps / lead.**
- **Data analyst → economy or monetization design.**
- **Community / support → LiveOps or CRM.**
- **Playable-ad / creative developer → prototyping / game design** (you already build tiny games whose objective has to land in 3 seconds).
- **Artist or programmer → designer** (Rogers' own path, from pixel artist to designer).
- **Indie / jam games → portfolio → junior designer.** Ship small, finished games. That's the modern "preparation" Rogers talks about.
- **Titles ladder (varies by studio):** Junior Game Designer → Game Designer → Senior → Lead → Principal / Game Director.

**🪝 Make it stick: the Live-Game Theme Park.** The **game designer** builds the rides. The **level designer** lays the track for each coaster. The **systems and economy designers** run the ticket and token economy so it never inflates. The **LiveOps designer** throws the parade every weekend. The **monetization designer** runs the gift shops (tastefully, please). The **PM** reads the attendance numbers and decides what to build next. The **data analyst** counts every footstep. **UA** buys billboards on the highway. **QA** checks every bolt before the gates open. And the **game director** rides everything and asks, "Is it fun?"

## 🏆 Universal Truths & Clever Ideas
Level 1's end-of-chapter list, in my own words:
1. **A game is an activity with rules and a victory condition** (and at least one player).
   📱 *So for F2P:* every mode, event and level should pass the player + rules + victory test. If an event has no clear way to win, it's just a chore.
2. **Keep your game's objective simple, as simple as a 1950s board-game box.**
   📱 *So for F2P:* it has to fit a store subtitle, a 3-second ad hook and one tutorial line.
3. **Genres come in all shapes and sizes, so don't be afraid to mix and match them.**
   📱 *So for F2P:* mobile hits are usually hybrids (match-3 + decoration, board game + social events, roguelite + one-thumb shooter).
4. **Gaming technology keeps improving: adapt or get left behind.**
   📱 *So for F2P:* touch input, app stores, F2P, LiveOps tools and privacy changes like ATT each reshaped the market. Learn the new rules fast.
5. **It takes all kinds of people to make video games.**
   📱 *So for F2P:* add LiveOps, economy, monetization, data, UA and community to Rogers' list, and learn to speak each of their languages.

8. Elaborate cabinets needed floor space and were expensive to maintain, and home systems caught up with and passed arcade graphics in the late 1990s. Video games were replaced by redemption machines (claw games) and skill games like Skee-Ball. Cabinets went to collectors.
9. A multi-purpose controller (enough buttons, triggers and analog controls for many games) and swappable media (cartridges, CDs, DVDs) instead of a one-game motherboard.
10. Tetris on the Nintendo Game Boy (1989).
11. The keyboard allowed richer input, giving rise to text adventures (and longer sessions led to sims, construction/management and strategy). The mouse made the first-person shooter popular.
12. Expansion, exploration, exploitation, extermination.
13. Any four: level designers (paper maps, grey box, populating levels), system designers (how elements relate, like the economy or tech tree), scripters (trigger events like traps and camera moves via tools/code), combat designers (player-vs-enemy combat and balancing), creative director (vision, supervising designers).
14. Rewrite a weak story, write dialogue, clarify instructions and prompts, write the manual and fiction support material, and add "star power" to the box.
15. An unbiased outside view of the whole game that spots design and construction weaknesses and gives clear feedback or a new direction. Modern equivalents: publishing producers, greenlight reviews, outside eyes on soft-launch KPIs and builds.
16. The book's PM manages a studio's schedule, milestones, payments, legal contact and rating from the publisher side. The modern F2P PM owns the game's KPIs and roadmap, prioritizes features, and runs A/B tests: business owner of the live game.
17. Economy designer: currencies, sources and sinks, reward values, inflation and wallet health across segments. LiveOps designer: the event calendar, tournaments, seasons and passes, and their rewards and rhythm.
</details>
