# 🕹️ Press Start — Foreword, About the Author, Intro & Acknowledgments
> 📖 Book pp. xiii–xxi · ⏱️ ~20 min study · 📱 F2P relevance ★★★☆☆ — no mechanics yet, but it installs the designer mindset: fundamentals first, communicate visually, every screen is gameplay, games are a team sport.

## ⚡ TL;DR — the 30-second version
- The book is a **practical handbook of fundamentals**, not a theory text. Pull it off the shelf whenever you're stuck.
- Rogers' two **Very Important Things**: (1) **game designers have more fun**; (2) **nobody on your team wants to read your design**, so make it visual. He draws his design docs as cartoons, which is why the book is full of them.
- **Every chapter is about gameplay.** Even "passive" stuff like cutscenes and pause screens affects the player. (In F2P that also means the shop, popups, loading screens and the "out of lives" screen.)
- Games are made by **big teams**. "I designed it" is shorthand. Be nice, because the industry is small.
- Making games is **brutally hard** and also **the best job in the world**. If you want to make games, you have to love games.
- His examples lean toward **single-player action games**, but the principles carry over to other genres. This course does the F2P-mobile translation for you.

## 1. Foreword (by Danny Bilson)
**🧠 Big idea:** Bilson presents the book as an evergreen **handbook of fundamentals** for game creation, something you go back to whenever you need to reset to basics. His point is that fundamentals are where real inspiration comes from.

**📋 The details:**
- **Who:** Danny Bilson, Executive Vice President of Core Games at **THQ** (writing in February 2010). He comes back in Level 1 with his "board-game box" test for objectives (→ L01).
- **What the book is:** a general handbook that any designer can grab from the shelf, or open on a PC or e-reader, when they need the basics. Think of it as the game-design version of a **Scout Handbook** or a **Farmer's Almanac**.
- It has **clear, simple illustrations**, like those classic how-to books.
- **Why fundamentals:** every team that wants to win (sports teams, military units, any group effort) starts with the basics. After a big win, people almost always credit the fundamentals.
- A fundamental Bilson especially values in game design is **"doing one thing, well."** He says the book itself does exactly that: it has one job, fundamentals, and it does it.
- **Who it serves:**
  - **New designers**, starting out.
  - **Veterans stuck on a hard problem**: go back to fundamentals and they'll spark inspiration that takes you further than where you got stuck.

**🎮 Book examples:** None. It's a foreword. The analogies (sports teams, military units, Scout Handbook, Farmer's Almanac) all make the same point: basics are what you come back to.

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- "Do one thing well" is basically the first commandment of the **core loop**. Hit mobile games usually nail **one satisfying core action** before they pile on meta systems. A few examples:
  - Block Blast!: drop blocks, clear lines.
  - Royal Match: match-3 with very clean, juicy feedback.
  - Clash Royale: drop cards and take towers in short real-time battles.
- When a live game's numbers drop, good teams go **back to fundamentals** before adding features. They ask three questions. Is the core loop still fun? Is the **FTUE** clear? Is the player's goal obvious?

**🪝 Make it stick:** Fundamentals are your **checkpoint**. When the Design Boss wipes your party, you respawn at the basics, not at the fancy feature you were adding.

## 3. "If You Are Anything Like Me…" (the hook and the promise)
**🧠 Big idea:** Rogers opens by spoofing the cheap tricks books use on their first page. In doing that he shows the hook technique while promising a book that's honest, practical and fun.

**📋 The details:**
- He reads the first page of a book before buying it. If he likes page one, he'll probably like the whole book.
- **Parody #1, the action excerpt:** lots of books open with a thrilling excerpt to grab you. He writes an over-the-top one: a hero named Jack hacks through a zombie horde on a staircase, and Evelyn finally reaches the helicopter, only to admit she can't fly it. (Fun detail: Jack and Evelyn are also names in the Rogers family listed in the Acknowledgments.) Then he claims he'd *never* stoop to such cheap tactics.
- **Parody #2, the celebrity blurb:** books chase respectability with a glowing quote from a famous person. He prints a fake rave from "a very famous game designer," claiming that page one of the book taught more than **25 years** working in the industry. The footnote admits there's no such person… unless you count **Shigeru Miyamoto**, Mario's creator. Then he jokes he should have written the fake quote in Japanese.
- **Why both parodies?** They're two classic ways a first page tries to persuade you: *excitement* (the action excerpt) and *borrowed authority* (the endorsement). He then drops both and lets you judge the book on its own merit, since you're a discriminating reader who doesn't need someone else to tell you what to think.
- **The real pitch:** you're a discerning reader who wants the straight truth. The book covers the **who, what, where and, above all, how** of designing video games.
- **The A–Z teaser:** one long sentence strings together one topic per letter, from **A**rcade games to **Z**ombies. Along the way you get silly and serious items side by side: boss fights, deadly traps, ergonomics, haunted mansions, leitmotifs, NPCs, one-sheets, pitch sessions, smart bombs, un-fun, the Y-axis, and the **Triangle of Weirdness**, plus oddities like chili, giant hydras, killer bunnies, Mexican pizza, robotic chickens and whack-a-mole. If any of that interests you, this book is for you.

**🎮 Book examples:** The zombie/helicopter excerpt is the "spectacle hook" done badly on purpose. The fake Miyamoto-ish blurb is "borrowed authority."

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- Your **store page plus the first 30 seconds** are your "first page." Players decide fast whether to stay, so the hook matters as much as it does for a book.
- Many mobile games open with a **spectacle moment**: a flashy tutorial battle, a powerful "preview" hero, or a dramatic story beat. Then they scale the player back to level 1. That's the same trick Rogers is spoofing, and it works when it's honest about the game.
- The dark side is **misleading ads** that show gameplay that isn't in the game. Advertising regulators, for example in the UK, have ruled against some mobile game ads for this. The rule: hook hard, but make sure the game delivers what the hook promised.

**🪝 Make it stick:** The book opens with a zombie on page one and ends with chili at the back (the final chapter is literally titled "Exactly Like Making Chili"). The alphabet runs from **A**rcade to **Z**ombies.

## 4. Ground rules of the book
**🧠 Big idea:** Four rules shape how to read everything that follows. These are *his* methods. Games are team efforts. His examples lean toward action games. And gameplay is in every chapter.

**📋 The details:**
- **Many valid ways:** there are lots of approaches to game design, and they're all valid **as long as they communicate the designer's ideas**. What's in Level Up! is *his* way of doing it.
- **"I designed a game" is shorthand.** Games are made by many, many talented people (you meet them in Level 1). Claiming you did it all yourself is wrong *and* egotistical. There's no "I" in team. His footnote jokes that there is, however, a "me."
- **Career advice in a footnote:** the games industry is **small**. Nobody can afford to make enemies. Be a **nice, hardworking person** and you'll go far.
- **Genre bias:** most of the games he worked on were **single-player action games**, so his examples lean that way. He's found that most gameplay concepts **transfer across genres**, and translating them to your own genre shouldn't be hard.
- **No "gameplay chapter":** don't look for a single chapter on gameplay, because **every chapter is about gameplay**. Think about gameplay and its effect on the player *all the time*, even when you're designing passive things like **cutscenes and pause screens**.

**🎮 Book examples:** Cutscenes and pause screens are his examples of "passive" elements that still need gameplay thinking. Level 16 is all about cutscenes (→ L16).

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- **Everything on screen is gameplay**, and in F2P that list is long:
  - The **shop** and offer popups.
  - The **"out of lives / out of moves"** screen. In match-3 this is one of the most important screens in the game, because that's where players decide whether to quit, wait, watch an ad or pay.
  - **Loading screens**, which can carry tips or hints about upcoming events.
  - **Daily login calendars**, **push notifications**, and where you place **rewarded ads**.
- **Team-first culture matters even more in live games**, where designers, analysts, artists, LiveOps and UA (user acquisition) people coordinate every few weeks for years.
- Mobile studios vary just as much in design culture. Some are **data-led** and A/B test everything. Others are **creative-led** and protect the vision. Both are valid if they communicate and ship.

> ⚠️ **2010 → 2026:** His "single-player action game" lens doesn't map 1:1 to mobile F2P. Sessions are short (often a few minutes), input is touch, players get interrupted constantly (calls, notifications, train stops), and the business model shapes the design. The *principles* (clarity, feedback, pacing, fun) still hold, and each later module adds the mobile translation.

**🪝 Make it stick:** "There's no I in TEAM, but there *is* a ME." If it's on the screen, it's gameplay. Yes, even the loading bar.

## 5. The bad news (and the good news)
**🧠 Big idea:** Game development is very hard work, and it's also the best job in the world. Expect both at once.

**📋 The details:**
- **Bad news:** making video games is *very hard work*. That comes from someone with 16+ years in the industry and games that sold millions of copies.
- Footnote: a former boss used to wander the halls grumbling about what a hard business games are. Rogers laughed at the time. He doesn't anymore, because the boss was right.
- **Good news:** it's still the **best job in the world**. The job feels like a mix of thrilling and frustrating, rewarding and nerve-wracking, hectic and boring, even vomit-inducing, and yet just plain fun.

**🎮 Book examples:** Just his career experience: 16 years, millions sold, still calls it hard.

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- In F2P, **launch is the starting line, not the finish line.** A successful live game can run for many years (Clash of Clans launched in 2012 and is still live), with constant updates, events and balance changes.
- **Most F2P projects never make it past soft launch.** Studios routinely kill games whose early retention or monetization numbers aren't strong enough. Supercell is well known for openly killing many projects before global launch. That's normal, not a personal failure.
- The emotional roller coaster now comes with a **KPI dashboard**. Every update gets measured.

**🪝 Make it stick:** Game dev is a roller coaster you're building while you ride it. In F2P, you never get off.

## 6. No, You Can't Have My Job (Truths, Ideas and the two Very Important Things)
**🧠 Big idea:** Rogers boils his career down into reusable lessons. Every Level ends with **Universal Truths and Clever Ideas**, and there are two **Very Important Things** he wants you to learn first.

**📋 The details:**
- **Universal Truths & Clever Ideas:** lessons from his career, collected at the end of each Level for convenience. (In this course they appear in each module's 🏆 section and in the app's "truths" deck.)
- **Very Important Things** are printed in bold capitals so you know they matter.
- **Very Important Thing #1: GAME DESIGNERS HAVE MORE FUN.**
  - His first industry job was **artist** in the **16-bit** era, drawing everything pixel by pixel. Artists were nicknamed **"pixel pushers"** and **"sprite monkeys"**, and neither was a compliment.
  - He respects great pixel artists, naming **Paul Robertson** and the teams behind **Metal Slug** and the classic **Capcom fighting games**. But for him, pixel art felt like drawing with **bathroom tiles**.
  - *Visual:* the book shows three of his own pixel sprites, a small masked, antennae-helmeted hero in mid-action poses (running, punching). It's proof he really did push pixels.
  - While pushing pixels, he heard loud laughter from the designers' cubicles next door, peeked over the wall, and saw them having a great time. He wasn't. He decided that making games should be fun, that he wanted to have fun, and that he'd become a designer. He **worked his way up the ladder** and did it.
- **Very Important Thing #2: NO ONE ON YOUR TEAM WANTS TO READ YOUR DESIGN.**
  - It's a horrible discovery, and every designer needs to hear it. As a brand-new designer with brand-new design docs, he found that nobody would read them.
  - **His fix:** he started **drawing his design documents as cartoons**. It worked. His teammates understood the ideas, and he's designed that way ever since, including on top-selling titles.
  - That's why the book is full of cartoons: so you keep reading, understand the ideas, apply them, and become a great designer too.
  - *Visual:* a cheerful designer proudly holds up a "Game Design Document" next to a happy gamer, both holding controllers, with sunburst rays behind them. Design docs should feel like *that*, not like homework.
- **The principle under VIT #2:** a design document's job is **communication**, not archiving. If nobody reads it, it might as well not exist.

**🎮 Book examples:** Paul Robertson, Metal Slug and the classic Capcom fighters are the gold standard of pixel art. The book's own cartoons are VIT #2 in action.

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- Modern "cartoon design docs" include:
  - **One-page feature specs**: goal, player fantasy, flow, KPIs, open questions.
  - **Annotated mockups** in Figma or Miro, and **screen-flow diagrams** for events and stores.
  - **Short screen-recorded walkthroughs**, GIFs of a greybox, clickable prototypes.
  - **Economy numbers in a spreadsheet**, linked from the spec rather than pasted into it as walls of text.
- F2P teams also want **the "why" at the top**: which metric the feature is meant to move (for example, "a weekend event to lift D7 retention for mid-tier spenders") and how you'll know it worked.
- → L04 covers the paperwork in depth (one-sheets, ten-pagers, GDDs).

**🪝 Make it stick:** "Draw it or it didn't happen." Imagine your 40-page GDD being used as a doorstop while a napkin sketch gets pinned to the wall and actually built.

## 7. Who Is This Book For?
**🧠 Big idea:** Four kinds of reader: working pros who need practical fixes, aspiring designers who need a mentor, students who need to skip the author's mistakes, and anyone who loves games.

**📋 The details:**
1. **Working video game professionals.**
   - Many design books are mostly **theory**, which he's rarely found useful mid-project. Theory is great for conferences and "wine and cheese" industry mixers.
   - In the trenches (sleeves rolled up, figurative blood on the walls; his footnote assures us nobody has actually died making a game) you need **practical, nuts-and-bolts advice** for real problems.
   - He hopes pros will find the tips useful day to day, while the book still works for beginners.
2. **Future video game designers.**
   - The point of the pixel-pusher story is that **he was just like you**.
   - Maybe you're an **artist** tired of hearing designers laugh, a **programmer** convinced you could design a better enemy encounter than the person doing it now, or a **tester** who wants to move up but doesn't know how.
   - When he started, **no design books existed**. You learned from other designers. He was lucky to have **a mentor and an opportunity**.
   - If you have neither: **this book is your mentor.** Follow the advice, **be prepared**, and **grab the opportunity** when it finally comes.
3. **Students of game design.**
   - There were **no game design classes** when he started. He made it up as he went and made a lot of mistakes.
   - That's why he wrote the book: so you can learn from **his mistakes before they become yours**.
4. **Anyone who loves video games.**
   - He loves playing them, making them and reading about making them.
   - To make games, **you must love games.** He knows industry people who admit they don't like playing games, which makes no sense to him. They should step aside for someone who does love games: you.
- Sign-off: he asks if you're ready and launches into how games get made. (*Visual:* a cartoon of a scowling player hunched in a desk chair, hammering a controller in front of an old CRT TV. That intensity is what loving games looks like.)

**🎮 Book examples:** The career paths he names (artist → designer, programmer → designer, tester → designer) are exactly the routes people take into design.

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- **Common doors into F2P design:** QA tester, level designer on a puzzle game (match-3 studios often hire level designers who later move into systems or LiveOps), community or player support → LiveOps, data analyst → economy design, playable-ad or creative developer → gameplay prototyping.
- **"Love games" in F2P:** your players often aren't like you. They may play differently, spend differently and want different things. Loving games has to include **respecting games you wouldn't personally choose**. Study the top-grossing charts outside your taste and ask why millions of people love them.

> ⚠️ **2010 → 2026:** The "no books, no classes" world is gone. Today there are degrees, GDC Vault talks, F2P deconstruction blogs and podcasts, and design communities. The scarce things are now **practice and shipped work**, not information. His formula still holds: **a mentor (people or books) + preparation + taking the opportunity**.

**🪝 Make it stick:** The book's audience is an RPG party. 🛡️ **Veteran** (working pro), 🗡️ **Rookie** (future designer), 📚 **Apprentice** (student), 🎵 **Bard** (the fan who just loves games). Rogers is the quest-giver.

## 8. Acknowledgments: "Everything I Learned about Writing a Book"
**🧠 Big idea:** The one lesson he states outright is that **books don't write themselves**. Writers need support and inspiration from many people. The list of who he thanks shows how a creative career actually gets built.

**📋 The details:**
- He hopes the book was educational and inspiring, and that it's taught him that no one writes alone.
- **Who he thanks, by category** (not the whole list):
  - **Family**: many Rogers family members.
  - **Industry colleagues and friends**: including Danny Bilson (foreword) and Noah Stein (also the book's technical editor), plus THQ's legal department.
  - **Mentors**: William Anderson and David Siller, named explicitly as mentors.
  - **A reader's request**: Jeremiah Slackza, for asking for the **Platform Primer** (a section in Level 9, → L09).
  - **His first test audience**: Scott Frazier.
  - **The GDC 2008 selection committee** (his conference talk), **Disneyland's original Imagineers**, and the **Los Angeles customizing gang** (toy customizers).
  - **The publisher's editors** at John Wiley & Sons (Juliet Booker, Gareth Haman, Katherine Batchelor, Ellie Scott), especially Chris Webb, who made the phone call that started the deal.
  - **Friends, colleagues and relatives** named one by one (the list is long, e.g. Jackie Kashian, Tommy Tallarico, Andy Ashcraft, Paul Guirao). It's a real-world picture of how many hands touch one book, just as many hands touch one game.
  - **Cory Doctorow**, whose glowing review of his GDC talk **kicked off the whole book**. Rogers owes him a drink.
- Last line: thank you for buying the book, now go design great games, he can't wait to play them.

**🎮 Book examples:** Rogers' own path is the case study: GDC talk → public praise → publisher call → book.

**📱 F2P mobile translation:** These are lessons I'm drawing from his story, applied to *your* design career:
1. **Make it a team sport.** Even "solo" work needs reviewers, mentors and editors.
2. **Get a first test audience.** Playtest your docs and pitches the way you playtest games.
3. **Listen to requests.** A reader's request became a whole section. Player feedback becomes features in the same way.
4. **Share publicly.** One talk plus one review led to a book deal. Post your F2P teardowns and prototypes, because visibility creates opportunities.
5. **Find mentors.** He names two.
6. **Look outside games for inspiration.** Imagineers (theme-park design) and toy customizers both fed his design thinking (→ L09 level design borrows heavily from theme parks).

**🪝 Make it stick:** Books don't write themselves. Neither do games, design docs or careers. You need a party, not a solo run.

## 🎯 Side quests (5–15 min each)
1. **Napkin spec (10 min):** pick any feature from a F2P game you play (for example a daily login calendar). Explain it in **one drawing plus at most 5 labels**. No paragraphs allowed.
2. **Everything-is-gameplay hunt (10 min):** open a F2P game and screenshot 5 *non-gameplay* screens (shop, settings, loading, popup, out-of-lives). For each, write one line on how it changes what the player feels or does.
3. **Box test your favorite game (5 min):** write its objective in one sentence, as if printed on a board-game box. (Preview of L01.)
4. **First-page autopsy (10 min):** install a new F2P game and note what happens in the first 30 seconds. Is it a spectacle hook, a calm tutorial, or straight to play? Did it make you want page two?
5. **Party up (10 min):** list 3 people who could be your "first test audience" for design work, and 1 person or community that could act as a mentor. Message one of them.

## ⚔️ Main quest (homework)
**Quest A: "The Cartoon Spec."** Take one real feature from a F2P game (an event, a shop offer, a clan feature) and turn it into a **one-page visual spec** in the style of VIT #2.
- Include: a one-line goal, the player fantasy, a 4–6 panel flow (sketches or mockups), 3 key numbers (cost, reward, duration), the KPI it's meant to move, and 2 open questions.
- **Deliverable:** one PNG or PDF page.
- ✅ **Done when:** a friend who has never played the game can explain the feature back to you after 60 seconds of looking. Bonus: post it publicly as "Day 1 of learning game design."

**Quest B: "Launch your swipe file."** Set up your design journal (Notion, Obsidian or paper). Create a template with these fields: Game · Genre · Core loop in one sentence · Best thing · Worst thing · What I'd steal · F2P monetization notes. Fill it in for 3 F2P games from different genres.
- **Deliverable:** the template plus 3 filled entries.
- ✅ **Done when:** each entry has a one-sentence core loop and at least one "what I'd steal."

## 🔬 Play Lab
1. **Block Blast! (10 min):** look for the "one thing done well." What's the single core action, and what feedback (sound, animation, combo text) makes it satisfying? *Question:* if you removed everything except the core action, would it still be fun?
2. **Royal Match, or any match-3 (10 min):** play until you fail a level. *Question:* what exactly does the fail screen offer you (moves, ad, coins, retry), and how does it try to keep you playing? That's "every screen is gameplay" in action.
3. **Any newly released F2P game (10 min):** study the store page and the first minute. *Question:* does the opening hook honestly match the game you actually play by minute 5?

## 🗣️ Feynman check
In 60 seconds, explain to a non-gamer friend why "nobody on your team wants to read your design" is actually *good* news, and what a designer should do about it.
