# 🎬 Level 16 — Cutscenes, or No One's Gonna Watch 'Em Anyway
> 📖 Book pp. 407–416 · ⏱️ ~35 min study · 📱 F2P relevance ★★★☆☆ — mobile players skip almost everything, but short story beats, dialogue bubbles in narrative casual games, story-driven events and cinematic ads/trailers all run on this chapter's craft.

## ⚡ TL;DR — the 30-second version
- A cutscene is a non-playable sequence for story, spectacle, atmosphere, dialogue, character and clues. They look great but have a **bad reputation**: too long, unnecessary, a chore — so players **skip**.
- First ask: **could this be done in gameplay instead?** It's always better to have the player **do** than **watch**.
- Six ways to make one: **FMV, animated, Flash-animated, prerendered, puppet show, scripted event**. Pick what fits your **style, budget and schedule**.
- Scripted events need **staging** so the player actually sees them (trigger on look, frame with geometry, start from the player's view, show cause → effect).
- Write like a pro in **eight steps**: outline → scene breakdown → cutscene vs gameplay → write brief, funny dialogue → standard screenplay format → read aloud → let it simmer + get feedback → break lines into a VO spreadsheet with file names.
- Record a **temp track** for timing (never lip-sync to it), hire **professional actors** through a **voice director**, bring the right kit to the session, keep the writer in the room, welcome improv — and have fun.

## 1. What a cutscene is — and the question to ask first

**🧠 Big idea:** Cutscenes are a double-edged sword: gorgeous, but often long, pointless or boring, so players skip them. Before making one, ask whether the moment could happen **in gameplay** instead.

**📋 The details:**
- **Definition:** a clip (**animated** or **live-action**) in the game that exists to:
  - advance the **story**,
  - create **spectacle**,
  - provide **atmosphere**,
  - deliver **dialogue**,
  - develop **characters**,
  - and reveal **clues** the player might otherwise miss during play.
  - The player usually has **no control** while it plays.
- **Upside:** cutscenes usually look fantastic and can show your world and characters in ways the game engine **can't reproduce**.
- **Downside (the stigma):** a history of cutscenes that are **too long**, **unnecessary to the story**, or a **chore to watch**. Many players skip them (when the game allows!) to "get to the game."
- **Rogers' very important question:** **Can it be done in the game?** (He answers it in the writing steps: tell as much as possible through gameplay.)

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- Mobile sessions are short and interrupted; players open the app **to play**, not to watch. Every second of unskippable cinema in the FTUE costs installs that never reach level 3.
- The F2P version of "can it be done in the game?": can the story beat be a **level goal**, a **meta-task** ("fix the staircase"), a **character reacting on the board**, or a **one-line bubble** over gameplay?

**🪝 Make it stick:** The chapter title is the joke and the warning: assume **nobody** will watch. Design so the game still makes sense — and anyone who *does* watch gets a bonus.

## 2. Six kinds of cutscenes

**🧠 Big idea:** Like movies, cutscenes come in many production styles, each with different cost, look and interactivity. Choose the one that fits your game's style, budget and schedule.

**📋 The details:**
- Like film, cutscenes can be produced in many different ways; Rogers lists six.

| Type | What it is | Player control | Book examples | Pros / cons |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Full motion video (FMV)** | **Live-action** film with real actors, **popular when games first shipped on CD media**; usually shot by an **outside production company** with full movie resources | None (some games made it playable) | Wing Commander III: Heart of the Tiger (1994), Command & Conquer (1995), The Horde (1994) — Hollywood actors and production values. Mid-90s "interactive movie" systems (3DO, Philips CD-i, Sega Mega CD, PCs) ran FMV-heavy games like Night Trap (1992), Sewer Shark (1992), Phantasmagoria (1995), Psychic Detective (1995) | Big star power; but by 2010 seen as **too expensive** for something players **may not even watch** (even though DVDs could hold the video easily) |
| **Animated (full-motion animation)** | **Cel** or **stop-motion** animation converted to video the engine plays, used for **title and story** sequences | None | The Neverhood games (1996), The Curse of Monkey Island (1997), Professor Layton series (2007) | Gorgeous and distinctive; **rare** because production is involved, shooting is long and costly |
| **Flash-animated** | Sequences made in **Adobe Flash**: still images + simple movement give a **distinctive style**. Flash was often used for **animatics**, sometimes for final cutscenes | None | Sly Cooper series (2002), the first God of War | Cheap, stylish; limited motion |
| **Prerendered** | Built from **high-resolution** versions of the game's characters and environments with **cinematic cameras** for dramatic choreography and storytelling | None | Any Blizzard title, Final Fantasy series, Namco fighting games | With enough **money, time and manpower** they look spectacular (footnote: while Tekken 3 was in production, its Japanese producer proudly told Rogers the spectacular cinematics were the work of just two animators — and then that both ended up in the hospital from exhaustion: a warning about cost and crunch) |
| **Puppet show** | Uses **in-game assets** (characters, environments). Named for early versions where characters moved stiffly, like **marionettes**. Visually distinguished from gameplay only by the **cinematic camera** | None, or **limited** character/camera movement (e.g., looking around with the hero's head) | Assassin's Creed (2007), Call of Duty: Modern Warfare series | Cheap, consistent with the game's look; can't show what the engine can't (no 10,000-orc armies) |
| **Scripted event** | Also in-game assets, but the player keeps **limited to full interaction** during the sequence | Partial to full | Half-Life (1998), The Operative: No One Lives Forever (2000), Call of Duty series | The **preferred way** to tell story **without breaking gameplay pace**; common in FPS and action. Risks: the player **misses** it if poorly choreographed, and it gets **repetitive** if they must replay it after dying (the **"Groundhog Day" phenomenon** — stuck in a time loop) |

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**

| Book type | Mobile F2P descendant |
|---|---|
| FMV | Rare in-game; live-action shows up in **TV/online ads** (e.g., Clash of Clans' 2015 Super Bowl ad starring Liam Neeson) and interactive-story apps |
| Animated | **2D animated shorts** for marketing (Supercell's animated Clash series and trailers), event intros |
| Flash-animated | Flash is dead (Adobe ended support in 2020), but its spirit lives in **2D skeletal animation** tools (Spine, Live2D) used for characters and comic-style story panels |
| Prerendered | **CG launch trailers** and big-season trailers — mostly marketing, not in-game (download size!) |
| Puppet show | **In-engine story beats**: characters animate on the meta screen (Homescapes/Gardenscapes' butler Austin reacting to your renovations) |
| Scripted event | **FTUE tutorials**: the guided first battle or first level where the game "stages" events around the player's actions |

> ⚠️ **2010 → 2026:** Flash and the "interactive movie" consoles are history, but the cost logic is unchanged: **expensive cinema for players who skip it is a bad trade**. On mobile, heavy video also bloats the download — another reason prerendered video lives mostly in ads and trailers.

**🪝 Make it stick:** **"Film, Frames, Flash, Fancy render, Puppets, Play"** — FMV, animation frames, Flash, prerendered, puppet show, scripted (playable) event. From most Hollywood to most game.

## 3. Making sure players actually see your scripted event

**🧠 Big idea:** A scripted event the player doesn't see is wasted money. Stage it like a theme-park ride: control where they look and make cause and effect obvious.

**📋 The details — four staging techniques:**
1. **Trigger on gaze:** only start the event when the player character is actually **looking at / facing** it.
2. **Frame the scene with level geometry:** build the space (windows, corridors, openings) so the player gets a **clear view** of the action.
3. **Tour the space:** **move the camera** around the environment to show its **layout and space**.
4. **Start from the player:** if the camera moves, **start the event from the player's point of view or position** — especially for in-game-camera moments showing **geometry changing**, **puzzle hints**, or **enemy reveals**.
- **Always show clear cause and effect**: "pull this lever → that door opens."
- Once you know which storytelling device fits, the bad news: **now you have to write it** (→ Section 4).

**🎮 Book examples:** Half-Life, No One Lives Forever and Call of Duty (scripted events as pacing-friendly storytelling), the Groundhog Day problem (replaying scripted events after dying).

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- **Cause → effect is the FTUE's #1 job:** "tap this booster → watch it clear the row," "build the barracks → train troops." A short camera pan from the player's tap to the result teaches faster than any text.
- **Gaze triggers become "focus" overlays:** dim the screen except the element to tap, so the player can't miss the event.
- **Groundhog Day on mobile:** if a level intro or boss intro replays on every retry, players rage. **Show it once**, then skip automatically on retries.
- Pan the camera across a **new meta area** when it unlocks — the mobile version of "show the layout."

**🪝 Make it stick:** *"If a tree falls in a scripted event and the player was looking at the minimap, it didn't happen."*

## 4. How to Write a Screenplay in Eight Easy Steps

**🧠 Big idea:** Game writers are writing professionals, so write like one: outline, break down, choose what's playable, write short, use standard format, read aloud, rest and get feedback, then prepare lines for voice actors.

**📋 The details:**
- **Go deeper later:** Rogers recommends three classic screenwriting books (and jokes: read them *after* this one):
  - **Screenplay: The Foundations of Screenwriting** — Syd Field (1984)
  - **Story: Substance, Structure, Style and the Principles of Screenwriting** — Robert McKee (1997)
  - **Screenwriting 434** (a practical guide to step-outlining and writing your screenplay) — Lew Hunter (2004)
- His guide is the "quick and dirty" version, so your script can be used by **storyboard artists**, **cutscene animators** and **voice actors**.

### Step 1 — Outline your story
- Know your **beginning, middle and end**, or you won't know what you're writing. (Covered back in → **L03**.)

### Step 2 — Break it down scene by scene
- For each scene: **which characters** appear and **which location** it happens in. This matters for **staging** and **asset creation**.
  - Reality check: you may not be able to show 10,000 orc warriors charging over a hill in a **puppet-show** cutscene.
- **Play with scene order.** A **flashback** opening can work if a later scene has more action than your first.
  - **SpongeBob SquarePants: Atlantis SquarePantis** (THQ, 2007) **started at the end of the story** because it was the most action-packed scene and the team wanted a high-action opening.
- Rogers' preference: **start with a bang** to grab the audience.

### Step 3 — Decide what's a cutscene and what's gameplay
- Tell **as much story as possible through gameplay** — playing is what players do most.
- **Save cutscenes for big moments** — **kissing and blowing things up** — things the game engine **can't** support.
- **Don't make the player watch something they should be doing.** Rogers' very important thing: **"It's always better to have the player do than watch."**
- Quick test: if the player could perform the moment, don't film it.

### Step 4 — Write your scenes and dialogue
- Decide **what needs to happen** and **what needs to be said**.
- Communicate with **action** as much as (or more than) with words.
- **Write to entertain** — being **funny** doesn't hurt.
- Above all, be **brief** (Shakespeare's point that brevity is the soul of wit). No long yapping, no psycho/technobabble (his footnote pokes the Metal Gear Solid series).
- Get the point across in **as few words as possible**. Rogers treated dialogue like the old game show Name That Tune, where contestants bid ever fewer notes. His imagined version: one writer boasts "I can write that dialogue in **12 lines**," the other tops it with "**eight lines or less**," and the exchange ends with an exasperated "Write. That. Dialogue!" Compete with yourself to cut.

### Step 5 — Use the official screenplay format
- If you're writing a video game, you **are** a writing professional — so use the format every other entertainment professional uses. No need to reinvent the wheel.
- **The basic format, field by field:**

| Element | What goes there |
|---|---|
| **Scene heading** | `SCENE #. INT. or EXT. — LOCATION — TIME OF DAY` (interior or exterior; pick one) |
| **Camera angle** | The shot (e.g., WIDE SHOT, CLOSE ON, POV) |
| **Action / description** | Describe the setting; introduce **CHARACTERS IN ALL CAPS**; highlight **ACTION IN ALL CAPS** too |
| **Character name** | Who speaks (caps, centered in real scripts) |
| **Parenthetical** | (actor's direction in parentheses) |
| **Dialogue** | The spoken line — keep it brief |

- Software like **Final Draft** or **Movie Magic** handles the formatting (saves you mashing the Tab key).

*My own mobile-flavored example (not from the book):*
```
SCENE 3. INT. — DUSTY MANOR KITCHEN — MORNING

WIDE SHOT
Cobwebs everywhere. NANA ROSE, 70s, sharp-eyed, BLOWS dust
off a cracked teacup. A floorboard CREAKS behind her.

NANA ROSE
(not turning around)
If you're the plumber, you're three years late.
```

### Step 6 — Read your dialogue out loud
- Lines that sound great **in your head** often sound **strange or clunky** spoken. (Rogers recalls Harrison Ford's famous complaint to George Lucas that Star Wars dialogue could be typed but not said.)
- **Expect to rewrite — again and again.**

### Step 7 — Let it simmer
- Leave it for **a day or two**; you'll get new ideas and better ways to write scenes and lines.
- Have **someone else read it** and give feedback — and **don't hover** over them while they read.

### Step 8 — Prepare the script for voice actors (spreadsheet)
- Use a spreadsheet (the book suggests **Microsoft Excel**).
- **Break each character's dialogue out line by line** — that's how voice actors read and record.
- Isolated lines make the session faster for actor and director: no paging through a long script.
- **Keep scene numbers** on the sheet.
- Give **every line a file name**: the **sound engineer** uses it to name each audio file when cutting up the session recordings, and the **programmer** uses the same file to put the line in the game.

**🎮 Book examples:** SpongeBob: Atlantis SquarePantis (start at the end for a bang), Metal Gear Solid (too much babble), the Star Wars dialogue anecdote (read it aloud).

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- **Step 2's asset reality** is brutal on mobile: every character pose, background and animation adds download size and art cost. Count assets per scene.
- **Step 3 = the F2P golden rule:** the story should mostly live in **level goals, meta tasks and character reactions**. Reserve animated beats for **milestones** (new area unlocked, villain reveal, season finale).
- **Step 4 on a phone screen:** a dialogue bubble should fit **one breath** — roughly one short sentence. Three bubbles max before the player gets to tap something.
- **Step 6 for text-only games:** still read bubbles aloud — clunky text reads clunky too.
- **Step 7 = playtest the story:** watch 5 testers; count how many tap "skip" and when.
- **Step 8 = localization pipeline:** the per-line file name becomes the **string ID / localization key** (e.g., `ev_winter_ch1_nana_03`). Translators, VO studios and engineers all work from that one sheet.

**🪝 Make it stick:** The 8 steps as a road trip: **O**utline the route → **B**reak it into stops → **C**hoose which stops you drive vs just watch from the window → **W**rite short postcards → **F**ormat them properly → **R**ead them aloud → **R**est overnight → **S**preadsheet the souvenirs. *"Only Brave Cats Write Fast, Read Really Slowly."*

## 5. The VO script spreadsheet — and how to write a grunt

**🧠 Big idea:** Voice actors read exactly what's written, line by line. A clean spreadsheet with file names and notes, and grunts spelled the way you want them to sound, saves time and gets the performance you want.

**📋 The details:**
- **The book's sample sheet** ("Cold Steele" VO script for the hero Jake Steele, actor TBD) has three columns:
  - **File Name** — a structured ID per line: opening-cinematic lines numbered by scene and line (Opening_01_01…), cutscene lines (Cutscene_01_01…), then in-game "barks" grouped by type: **climbing**, **collecting pickups/cash**, **drinking health**, **yelling during a charge move**, **victory**, **hit reactions**, **deaths**.
  - **Dialogue** — the exact line, including grunts and screams.
  - **Notes** — context and direction: which cinematic it belongs to, **what to emphasize**, what's happening (a character gets killed, Jake is slapped at the end of the line, "hurt but not defeated"), and labels for **alternate takes** (alternate climbing grunt, alternate charge move, alternate death).
- **Death and hit lines are specific:** separate takes for falling off a cliff, being shot and dropping to the knees, being burned, plus alternates.
- **VO = voice over.** VO scripts are full of screams and grunts. **Actors read them literally**, so **write it the way you want it to sound**: a short "Arg!" and a long "Aaaargh!" are completely different performances (one's a pirate, one's a death rattle).
- **Not sure how to spell a grunt?** Read **comic books** — full of **onomatopoeia** (words spelled like they sound: POW, CRASH, AIIEEE).

*My own mobile-flavored VO sheet (structure only, not from the book):*

| File Name | Dialogue | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| ev_winter_intro_01 | Brr! Who left the castle doors open? | Event intro bubble, playful, teeth chattering |
| hero_ember_attack_01 | Hyah! | Short attack bark, light effort |
| hero_ember_attack_02 | Hyaaah! | Alt take, bigger swing |
| hero_ember_hit_01 | Oof! | Light hit |
| hero_ember_ko_01 | Nooo… not… yet… | Knocked out, fading |
| hero_ember_win_01 | Too easy! | Victory, cocky |

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- Most casual F2P games use **little or no full VO** (cost × number of languages × download size). Instead: **short barks** (one-word reactions), **gibberish "voice" sounds**, or an **announcer** (Candy Crush's combo voice).
- **Barks are the in-game part of the sheet** and the part that matters most on mobile: attack, hit, victory, summon/reveal lines in gacha RPGs.
- Group file names by **character → context → variant** so LiveOps can add new skins/voices without chaos.

**🪝 Make it stick:** *"Arg is a pirate; Aaaargh is a funeral."* Spell your screams on purpose.

## 6. Temp tracks, voice actors, voice directors & the session

**🧠 Big idea:** Record a cheap **temp track** for timing, then hire **professionals** through a **voice director**, arrive prepared, keep the writer in the room, welcome improvisation — and enjoy it.

**📋 The details:**

**The temp track**
- After writing and breaking out the script, record a **temp track**: moderately talented amateurs and team members (like you) read the lines to work out **audio file size and length**.
- **You need:** the script, a willing actor, a decent microphone, recording software on a computer, and a **quiet, non-echoing** room.
- Read lines **the way you want the professional to perform them**.
- **Never animate lip sync to a temp track** — the real actor's performance will be very different. Use temp audio **only for timing and blocking**.

**Hire professional actors**
- You *could* voice the hero yourself, but **hire a professional** for the final game. Rogers has worked with dozens of VO actors: the gap between an actor and an amateur is **huge**. Hire one (or two, or a dozen).
- But **first hire a voice director**.

**What a voice director does**
- **Casts** your characters from your descriptions — be **accurate, not vague**. If helpful, name an **existing actor** you imagine in the role — you might even get them (footnote: Tim Schafer thought he'd never land Jack Black for Brütal Legend, and did).
- **Books the studio** and gets the best **rate**.
- **Schedules** to get the most recording time out of the actors.
- **Directs** the actors during sessions and works with **sound technicians** for the best quality.

**Before and during the session**
- Send the script to the voice director ahead of time. It **doesn't have to be final**, but warn them you'll bring changes.
- **Bring to the session:**
  1. **Extra printed copies** of the script.
  2. A **highlighter** — to mark lines for the actor during the session.
  3. A **pen or pencil** — for notes and tracking script changes (there **will** be changes).
  4. **Images of the characters** — whatever shows what each looks like. Actors often voice **several parts**, so even a minor background creature (his example: an unnamed drooling alien) deserves a picture. Send them to the voice director early too, to help casting.
  5. A **book or handheld game** — showbiz runs on "hurry up and wait"; there's lots of downtime while actors and technicians prep. Stay out of the way, but don't wander far.
  6. **Drinks and snacks** — sessions can last all day or all week, and just listening all day is exhausting.
- **The writer/designer must be present.** Rewrites happen constantly, and actors and the voice director don't always know a line's **context**. If you need something specific, **don't leave it to someone else**.
- **Improvisation is welcome:** giving actors freedom to add lines and alternate takes gives you **more material**, actors **enjoy** it, and if they enjoy working with you, they'll **want to work with you again**.
- **Have fun!** You're being paid to hear actors perform lines **you** wrote.

**📱 F2P mobile translation:**
- **Temp VO today** is often text-to-speech or AI voice for timing and prototypes. Same rule as the book: **timing only** — never lock animation or ship it without checking rights, and be aware voice-actor unions have negotiated rules about AI voice use.
- **Localization multiplies sessions:** each language needs casting, direction and context. Your character images and notes column become essential for every studio.
- **Barks for gacha heroes:** a hero's voice lines are part of what players collect and pay for — professional performance directly affects how desirable a character feels.
- **Improvised alternates** = free variants for barks, so players don't hear the same "Victory!" 500 times.

**🪝 Make it stick:** The session survival kit: **"Copies, Colors, Pen, Pictures, Pastime, Provisions"** — scripts, highlighter, pen, character images, book/game, snacks.

> ⚠️ **2010 → 2026:** The craft advice holds completely. What changed: remote recording is normal, AI voices exist (with legal and ethical strings attached), and mobile games often skip full VO entirely for size and localization reasons.

## 7. 📱 F2P Deep Dive — cutscenes on a phone
*(Not in the book — the 2026 mobile layer.)*

**🧠 Big idea:** On mobile, story is a **seasoning**, not the main course. Keep beats tiny, skippable and playable, push big cinema into **marketing**, and use narrative to give LiveOps events meaning.

### 7a. The skip button is not optional
- **Always skippable**, ideally from the first frame, and **remember** what the player has seen (never replay on retry — the Groundhog Day rule).
- Offer a **recap** ("Previously…") or a **story log/gallery** so skippers can catch up later.
- Watch analytics: **skip rate per beat** and **drop-off right after a cutscene** tell you which beats hurt.

### 7b. Sub-10-second story beats
- Rule of thumb (a design guideline, not a law): keep in-game story beats **under ~10 seconds** or **2–3 bubbles** before the player gets control back.
- **Do > watch:** turn exposition into a **tap**: the player taps to open the letter, place the first piece of furniture, fire the first cannon.

### 7c. Dialogue bubbles in narrative casual games
- Home-renovation match-3 games (Homescapes/Gardenscapes with Austin the butler) and merge-mystery games (Merge Mansion) deliver story through **short character bubbles** between levels and tasks — the mobile **puppet show**.
- Interactive-story apps (e.g., Choices: Stories You Play, Episode) make dialogue the core loop, with choices as the gameplay.
- **Writing rules from Step 4 apply double:** short, funny, action over words, one idea per bubble.

### 7d. Story-driven LiveOps
- Events get a **mini-story**: a villain arrives, a festival needs saving, a new chapter of the mansion mystery opens. Story gives players a **reason** to care about this week's event goal.
- **Season arcs** (battle-pass seasons, chapter updates) work like TV seasons: a teaser, weekly beats, a finale. Gacha RPGs often ship new story chapters with version updates.
- **Start with a bang** (Step 2) → open each event with its most dramatic moment.

### 7e. Cinematic trailers & ad creatives
- **Prerendered/animated cinema lives here:** CG launch trailers, animated shorts and character teasers build brand (Supercell is famous for animated Clash trailers and shorts).
- **Ad creatives are tiny cutscenes:** hook in the first 1–3 seconds (start with a bang), clear cause → effect, readable muted (captions).
- **Ethics and rules:** ads that show "gameplay" that isn't in the game have drawn regulator rulings (e.g., the UK advertising regulator has ruled against misleading game ads). Show what players will actually play.

### 7f. FTUE = scripted events
- The guided first level/battle is a **scripted event**: staged around the player, gaze-locked with focus overlays, showing obvious cause → effect, and **never longer than it must be**.

**🪝 Make it stick:** *"Snack-size story, feast-size trailer."*

## 🏆 Universal Truths & Clever Ideas
1. **Let your game's style, budget and schedule decide which kind of cutscene you make.** → 📱 so for F2P: bubbles and in-engine beats in-game; save CG for trailers.
2. **Learn the standard screenplay layout and write your script in it.** → 📱 so for F2P: and a line-by-line sheet with string IDs for localization.
3. **Open your game with a bang to hook the audience.** → 📱 so for F2P: the first 30 seconds of the FTUE and the first 3 seconds of every ad.
4. **Doing beats watching: tell your story through play wherever you can.** → 📱 so for F2P: turn exposition into taps, tasks and level goals.
5. **Always let players skip cutscenes, and never force the same one on them again and again.** → 📱 so for F2P: skip from frame one, no replays on retry, a story log for catch-up.
6. **Keep cutscenes short: it respects the player's time and saves your budget.** → 📱 so for F2P: aim for sub-10-second beats; shorter also means smaller downloads.
7. **Cutscenes are for kissing and explosions — grand spectacle and intimate moments; handle everything else through gameplay.** → 📱 so for F2P: milestones only — chapter unlocks, villain reveals, season finales.
8. **Hire professional actors when you can; they transform the result.** → 📱 so for F2P: especially for collectible hero barks.
9. **Give each actor their own isolated line sheet before voice sessions.** → 📱 so for F2P: one sheet, one ID per line, notes for context — for every language.

## 🎯 Side quests (5–15 min each)
1. **Skip-o-meter (10 min):** Install or open 2 F2P games and time every story beat in the first 10 minutes. How many are skippable? Which ran longer than 10 seconds?
2. **Do, don't watch (10 min):** Take one cutscene idea ("the dragon burns the village") and redesign it as 3 different playable beats on a phone.
3. **Format drill (15 min):** Write a 1-page scene in standard screenplay format for a mobile event intro (scene heading, camera, CAPS for characters/action, parenthetical, brief dialogue).
4. **Name That Tune (10 min):** Write a 10-line dialogue exchange, then cut it to 6 lines, then to 3 bubbles, without losing the point.
5. **Grunt dictionary (5 min):** Spell 10 different reactions (light hit, heavy hit, fall, victory…) so an actor would perform each correctly. Use comics onomatopoeia as inspiration.
6. **VO sheet (15 min):** Build a 15-line VO spreadsheet (File Name / Dialogue / Notes) for one gacha hero: summon, attack, skill, hit, KO, victory, with alternates.
7. **Scripted event staging (10 min):** Sketch a tutorial moment on a phone screen and annotate how you'd guarantee the player sees it (focus overlay, camera pan, cause → effect).

## ⚔️ Main quest (homework)
**Write the story package for one LiveOps event in a narrative casual game.**
- Deliverables: (1) a 5-beat event story outline (start with a bang, finale on the last day); (2) a beat sheet marking which beats are **gameplay** vs **bubble** vs **short animated beat**, each under ~10 seconds; (3) one key scene in standard screenplay format; (4) a VO/text spreadsheet with string IDs, dialogue and notes for every line; (5) a 15-second ad-creative storyboard (6 panels) that works muted.
- ✅ **Done when:** every beat has a "could this be gameplay?" answer; no in-game beat exceeds ~10 seconds or 3 bubbles; all lines have IDs and notes; you read the dialogue aloud and revised it at least once; you've posted the storyboard publicly (#gamedev #buildinpublic) and asked "would you skip this?"
